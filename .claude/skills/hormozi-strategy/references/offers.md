# $100M Offers — Operating Reference

**What this file is for.** The offer-construction layer: pick a market, set a price, engineer value up to that price, then enhance. Use it when the question is *what are we selling and for how much* — not traffic (Leads) or payment sequencing (Money Models). Rules, thresholds and diagnostics only. Every number was re-checked against the book's own text in a later verification pass; corrections and unsourceable claims are flagged inline, and the honest gaps are listed under **Coverage limits** at the end.

## 1. Order of operations

**Rule.** Run his sequence: **I** market → **II** niche → **III** price → **IV** value maximization → **V** offer components → **VI** trim & stack → **VII** scarcity → **VIII** urgency → **IX** bonuses → **X** guarantees → **XI** name.

Two sequencing rules carry the weight:
1. **Price is set before the offer is built** (III precedes V). Pick the premium price, then engineer value up to it. Cost-plus off current delivery is the default error.
2. **VII–XI are amplifiers, not substitutes.** On a weak core they accelerate the failure.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Did we choose the price before or after deciding what to deliver?
- Are we reaching for a bonus or a deadline because the core offer is weak?

## 2. Growth levers and the two constraints

**Rule.** Three ways to grow; every offer decision must move one.

```
Revenue = (# customers) × (average ticket) × (purchase frequency)
```

The two constraints a Grand Slam Offer breaks: **not enough clients**, **not enough cash**. Both trace to one root — prospects can price-compare you, so margin can't fund acquisition.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Which lever has moved in 90 days, and by how much?
- Is the binding constraint clients or cash?

## 3. The Grand Slam Offer

**Rule.** Build a bundle with no comparison set, then price where the market has no reference point to reject it. The decision becomes yes-or-no, not you-or-them.

**Five parts:** attractive promotion · unmatchable value proposition · premium price · unbeatable guarantee · a money model (payment terms) that lets you get paid to acquire customers.

**Signature maxim:** offers "so good people feel stupid saying no."

**His worked comparison** (a lead-gen agency selling to brick-and-mortar businesses; he says the figures are rounded but real). Old way: $1,000 down plus a $1,000/mo retainer — indistinguishable from every other agency, so the purchase is price-driven. New way: pay once, no retainer, you just cover ad spend, I generate *and* work the leads, you only pay when people show up, and I guarantee 20 people in month one or month two is free.

```
same ad spend           $10,000
response rate           × 2.5   (more compelling offer, same eyeballs)
close rate              × 2.5
front-end price         × 4
cash collected up front "22.4x"  ($5,000 → $112,000)
ROAS                    0.5:1 → 11.2:1
```
*Verified against the book's own wording: the three multipliers are 2.5 × 2.5 × 4 and he labels the product **22.4x**. Note 2.5 × 2.5 × 4 = 25, so his headline number does not reconcile with his own inputs — quote it as his figure, not as arithmetic. The widely-repeated "close rate 16% → 37%" comes from a chart image, not the prose, and could not be verified; use 2.5x.*

The maximum offer costs the same effort to build as a weak one. **Does not apply to** commodity arbitrage with no service layer, price-regulated categories, or anywhere you can't deliver a differentiated outcome — there it just accelerates refunds.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Can a prospect get three comparable quotes in ten minutes? Then you have a price, not an offer.
- If we doubled price tomorrow with no delivery change, what sentence would a prospect use to refuse?

## 4. Market selection — starving crowd and the four indicators

**Rule.** Market beats offer beats persuasion. Strength on a higher tier compensates for weakness below it; weakness above cannot be rescued from below.

- Starving market + weak offer + weak persuasion → still works
- Normal market + strong offer + weak persuasion → works *(target state)*
- Normal market + normal offer + elite persuasion → grinding
- Shrinking market + anything → fails

**Score all four; failing one disqualifies:**
1. **Massive pain** — desperation, not desire. Price scales with pain intensity; naming the suffering accurately does most of the selling.
2. **Purchasing power** — resume writing sold to the unemployed is maximum pain, easy targeting, no budget. Sold to executives it works.
3. **Easy to target** — lists, associations, trade groups, ad audiences. Needle-in-haystack prospecting eats the business on execution cost.
4. **Growing** — baseline ~9%/yr; outpacing needs 20–30%. The indicator operators skip.

*(Sources differ: one low-reliability derivative drops "growing." Four is correct.)*

**Three master markets:** health, wealth, relationships. Everything else is a subgroup.

**Paraphrased example.** An operator selling software into newspapers — a category shrinking ~25%/yr — had a risk-free revenue-share model and strong sales skill and still couldn't grow. Same skills redeployed into pandemic-era mask manufacturing hit millions per month in about five months.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Is underlying demand growing, flat, or shrinking year over year?
- Name three lists or ad audiences holding the buyer at >70% density. Can't? Targetability fails.

## 5. Niching and the price ladder

**Rule.** Narrow the avatar three to four levels and price rises roughly 100x for substantially the same content, because specificity reads as *built for me*.

```
Generic time management                            $19
…for sales professionals                           $99
…for B2B outbound sales reps                      $499   ← he calls this a 25x lift
…for B2B outbound power-tool & garden reps  $1,000–$2,000
```
*Corrected against the book text. The fourth rung is stated as roughly $1,000–$2,000, **not** $1,997. The $1,997 figure that circulates in summaries belongs to a **separate** example he gives immediately after: a generic weight-loss program at $19 versus the same program marketed only to shift nurses at $1,997. That second pair is where the ~100x claim comes from, and he poses it as a question ("if you can sell the same product for 100x, should you?"), not an instruction.*

Ladders: health → weight loss → post-childbirth weight loss → …on keto. Wealth → real estate → flipping → …sight-unseen, over DMs. Relationships → dating → dating over 60 → …without awkward first dates.

**The $10M threshold.** Below ~$10M/yr, niching down almost always raises profit; above it, expansion depends on verified TAM (total addressable market). **Verified as his own numbers:** reaching $10M/yr is a top-0.4% achievement, about 1 in 250 businesses, so the advice applies to 99.6% of readers. He also notes many companies pass $30M/yr inside a single niche — most "market ceilings" are execution ceilings.

**Paraphrased example.** His gym program targeted micro-gym owners: ~$16,000 for a 16-week done-with-you intensive, with ~35% upgrading into a three-year agreement at ~$42,000/yr. The market's low-price competitors charged ~$500/month and the single high-price competitor ~$5,000, so he priced at 3x the priciest and 32x the cheapest. Justification: the average client added roughly **$239,000/yr** in top line, against an average gym owner take-home of ~$35,280/yr — so many were committing half their annual pay or more. *(Corrected: the $239,000 is an annual figure, not "in 11 months.")*

**Niche-slap rule.** Don't market-hop. Switching after one failed offer resets you to zero; only a dying market justifies it. His own counsel on persistence is blunt: try a hundred offers and you will succeed — the failure mode is quitting the market, not running out of offers.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- How many levels down is the avatar, and what's the next level?
- Who are we accepting that we should be refusing?

## 6. The commodity problem

**Rule.** A commodity is anything sold on price comparison. The escape is not a better version of the same thing — it's a category of one.

The escape route is **psychological, not logical**: assume every logical improvement in your category has already been tried and arbitraged away.

**His definition:** a commodity is a product available from many places, so it gets bought on price rather than value. If a prospect compares you to an alternative and thinks *these are basically the same, I'll take the cheaper one*, they have commoditized you. Note this is a **perception** test, not a market-structure test — you are a commodity the moment the buyer can line you up against someone else.

**The cheapest-position rule.** His actual claim is that there is no strategic benefit to being the cheapest in a market, but there *is* one to being the most expensive. *(Correction: the "be ~10x cheaper or don't compete on price" line commonly attached to this book is not in it. It appears in his later material. Treat it as Hormozi-adjacent, not $100M Offers doctrine — the book's position is simply "don't play cheapest.")*

**Three differentiation paths** *(single-sourced, lower reliability):* change what you sell, who you sell to, or how you sell it.

**Paraphrased examples.** A subway system raised rider satisfaction more with arrival-countdown boards than faster trains would have — same objective wait, different perceived wait, millions instead of billions. Supplements (~$123B) are roughly double health clubs (~$62B) despite worse health outcomes, because they score better on time and effort.

**Limit:** the premium-only doctrine ignores volume-plus-low-price winners; his model targets small service businesses.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- What improvement is everyone racing on, and what psychological variable is nobody touching?
- Can a prospect build an apples-to-apples spreadsheet of us vs. two competitors? What would break it?

## 7. Pricing — the price-to-value discrepancy

**Rule.** Raise price until it causes a visible pause, then raise value to fit. Never close the gap by cutting price.

Three checks: be expensive enough that the prospect pauses; treat high price as a value signal (identical wine rates better when priced higher; priced placebos outperform cheap ones); and recognize that higher prices produce more committed clients and better results, making premium pricing the ethical choice.

```
Charge up to  100x  what fulfillment costs you   (his words — VERIFIED)
Never charge more than the product is worth      (his stated ceiling)
```
**Verified.** The 100x-of-cost rule is genuine book doctrine, not summarizer contamination: he tells you to abandon notions of "what's fair," points at phone carriers, pharma and media selling for enormous multiples of marginal cost, and says to think up to a hundred times what it costs to fulfill — not two or three times. The only limit he names is that you should never charge more than the thing is actually worth to the buyer.

*Still unsourced: the "deliver ~10x the price in value" ratio and the $100-cost → $10,000-value → $1,000-price worked example are reader constructions, not text from this book. Same for the 5–7x stack-to-price ratio. Use them as heuristics and label them as such.*

The operative test is the price-to-value **discrepancy**, not a ratio: if the outcome is worth far more to the buyer than the price, the gap does the selling.

**Virtuous cycle:** raise price → clients more invested → they do the work → better results → better testimonials and referrals → sales conviction rises → margin funds better fulfillment → justifies the price.
**Vicious cycle:** cut price → margin drops → less spend per client → quality drops → bad reviews → conviction drops → cut again.

The people who pay the most pay the most attention.

**Paraphrased examples.** A photography business he holds equity in took average ticket from ~$300 to ~$1,500 **over two years** — a 5x price move that lifted weekly profit from ~$1,000 to ~$38,000 (38x, because the increment was near-pure margin), while spending *less* time per customer and posting higher satisfaction. A medical practice raised price 50% with no service change and tripled profit in three months while acquiring *more* customers.

**Does not apply** before the differentiated offer exists — raising price on a naked commodity kills volume.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- What share of prospects say yes without hesitation? A high share means underpriced.
- Are we discounting to close, and what has that done to fulfillment spend per customer?

## 8. The Value Equation

**Rule.** Perceived value is the two things they want over the two things they don't.

```
            Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement
Value  =  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
            Perceived Time Delay × Perceived Effort & Sacrifice
```
*(Sources differ: one summary uses addition between the pairs. Multiplication is correct and load-bearing — any variable at zero zeroes the whole thing.)*

Value approaches infinity as the denominator approaches zero. **Small reductions in time or effort buy more perceived value than equivalent increases at the top** — that's the untapped leverage.

1. **Dream outcome — increase.** Sell the destination, not the vehicle. The deepest driver is **status**: to be seen as beautiful, respected, powerful, loved, higher in the hierarchy. A golf club that adds distance is sold as your friends' reaction, not physics.
2. **Perceived likelihood — increase.** Testimonials, case studies, statistics, track record, credentials, demonstrated process, guarantees, and what you deliberately *exclude*. A surgeon with 10,000 procedures commands a premium over one doing their first.
3. **Time delay — decrease.** Two layers: the long-term outcome bought, and short-term wins that keep them believing. Engineer a fast first win — his clients closed a first ~$2,000 sale inside 7 days. The only thing that beats free is fast.
4. **Effort & sacrifice — decrease.** Both what they must start doing and what they must stop. Done-for-you over done-with-you over DIY; tools over trainings; remove steps and decisions.

**The four prospect questions it answers:** What do I get? How do I know it'll happen? How long will it take? What's expected of me?

**Paraphrased scoring.** Meditation and an anti-anxiety prescription promise the same outcome; meditation scores well on outcome but poorly on likelihood and effort (~1.5/4), the prescription near-maximum (4/4) — a multi-billion-dollar category versus a free practice. Same structure explains a gym at ~$100/month versus a cosmetic procedure at $25–50K: identical dream outcome, opposite denominators.

**Limit.** This is *perceived* value; it can't rescue a product that materially fails, only relocate the failure into refunds.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Score the offer 0–1 on each driver. Which is weakest, and what's the cheapest intervention on it?
- How many days to a customer's first tangible win, and can that be halved?

## 9. Divergent thinking

**Rule.** Offer creation needs divergent thinking (one problem, many valid solutions), not the convergent thinking schooling trains.

Prime it with the brick exercise: two minutes listing uses for a brick. Most stall in single digits; varying attributes systematically — size, material, shape, quantity, state — yields 30+. Transfer that to *how* the solution is delivered. The constraint is imagination, not resources.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Can we list 15 delivery mechanisms for our biggest customer problem in two minutes?
- Are our "options" different vehicles, or one product at three price points?

## 10. Offer creation — five steps

**Rule.** Start at the destination, exhaustively enumerate what blocks it, invert each blocker, invent delivery vehicles, then cut and bundle.

**1. Define the dream outcome.** Concrete and measurable. Not a gym membership; the body.

**2. List every problem** — exhaustively, in the order the customer hits them: every intricate step, everything before and after the goal, limiting beliefs, and the *next* problem after this one. Ask of each: how do we make it worth it, easier, faster, more enjoyable and believable?

Every objection maps to one Value Equation driver: financial ("won't be worth it") · likelihood ("won't work for me") · effort ("too hard") · time ("takes too long"). Run this across each action sequence — for weight loss: buying food, cooking, exercising, socializing — and you generate **32–64 discrete problems**. The volume is the point; exhaustiveness is what makes the offer uncopyable.

**3. Invert each problem into a solution.** Template: *how to [outcome] without [pain] even if [biggest obstacle]*. Name each one. Repetition is fine — one solution often kills several problems. **Hard rule: solve all of them.** One unaddressed obstacle kills the purchase.

**4. Generate delivery vehicles** with the Delivery Cube. **5. Trim, then stack.**

*(Sources differ on step count: derivatives renumber to 6, 9 or 11 by folding in the enhancers. Five is correct.)*

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Have we written 30+ problems, or 5?
- Does the offer solve the problem that arrives *after* the one we're paid to solve?

## 11. The Delivery Cube

**Rule.** Vary every solution across six dimensions before choosing how to deliver.

He calls these his product **delivery cheat codes** — six questions asked of *every* solution on the list, deliberately, because an answer to one problem usually suggests answers to others.

| # | Dimension | Options |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Level of personal attention | 1-on-1 · small group · one-to-many |
| 2 | Level of effort expected of them | DIY · done-with-you (you teach) · done-for-you |
| 3 | If **live**, what medium? | in person · phone · email · text · Zoom · chat |
| 4 | If **recorded**, how is it consumed? | audio · video · written |
| 5 | Response speed & availability | 24/7 vs. 9–5; which days; reply within 5 min / 1 hr / 24 hr |
| 6 | 10x / 1-10th test | At 10x my price (say $100,000) what would I provide? At a tenth, how would I make it *more* valuable and still get them the result? |

*Corrected against the book: dimensions 3 and 4 are two separate questions (live medium vs. recorded format), not one "support type" axis. "Expert vs. standard staff" is not one of his six.*

The 10x/1-10th test is the most generative: 10x forces the premium tier you aren't selling, 1-10th forces scalable one-to-many substitutes. **Preferred landing zone — his explicit advice:** if you focus on one type of vehicle, make it a high-value **one-to-many** solution, because that carries the widest gap between what it costs you and what it's worth.

**Worked shape of the exercise.** For a single problem ("buying healthy food is hard and confusing"), he generates six 1-on-1 vehicles, four small-group vehicles and six one-to-many vehicles — in-store shopping trips, personalized lists, full-service shopping, text support while shopping, a live-streamed store tour, a recorded tour, a DIY calculator, pre-built delivery carts, a shopping buddy system. One problem, sixteen vehicles. Then you repeat it for every problem on the list.

**Sales/fulfillment inversion.** Easy-to-sell things are hard to fulfill and vice versa — which is why the easy-to-fulfill thing most operators pick won't sell.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Which dimensions have we never varied?
- What would the 10x version include, and could the 1-10th version become a bonus?

## 12. Trim and stack

**Rule.** Keep only high-value components. Among survivors, keep a handful of high-cost/high-value and make everything else high-value/low-cost.

*High value* = meaningful, fast, easy, believable, enjoyable. *High cost* = requires people to scale. *Low cost* = scales without people, at the price of upfront build effort.

```
                  LOW value to client    HIGH value to client
HIGH cost to you  cut first              keep — a handful only
LOW cost to you   cut second             KEEP — this is the core
```

Remove high-cost/low-value first, then low-cost/low-value, then score survivors against the Value Equation and bundle. The bundle must solve every listed problem and be impossible to line up against a competitor's price sheet.

**The scaling question to ask.** When the highest-value version is something you'd never actually do (his example: move in with the client and shop, cook and train for them), don't discard it — step back one notch at a time until you reach a version whose cost or time you can live with. Or keep it and raise the price until you *would* do it. The unscalable version is the anchor, not the answer.

**Start unscalable.** Begin with more high-cost/high-value than is comfortable to get yeses and cash moving, then systematize. Maxim: **create flow, monetize flow, then add friction.** His own path: 33 gym turnarounds in 18 months, flying out, fixing everything and relaunching in 21 days for a fee of 100% of the revenue he generated — averaging ~$42,000 added in those 21 days, peaking at eight gyms a month before the logistics broke him. The pivot came when he refused a trip and made the owner do the work himself: that gym collected ~$44,000 in 30 days, 4x its prior month. Same process, done-with-you instead of done-for-you, and travel stopped being the constraint — 4,000+ gyms followed.

**Paraphrased example.** A spreadsheet meal planner took ~100 hours to build once, then ~15 minutes per customer to personalize — it generated 100+ meals fitted to someone's macros, told them exactly what to buy and how to bulk-prep it. Premium custom plans at near-zero marginal cost.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Tag every component high/low value and high/low cost. What survives?
- What are we delivering manually that could be a 100-hour build and then 15 minutes forever?

## 13. Value stacking and price anchoring

**Rule.** A single offer is worth less than the identical offer decomposed into named, individually-priced components and stacked. Decomposition itself creates value.

**The mechanic, in his terms:** you anchor the stated price to the **core offer**, then each additional named bonus widens the price-to-value gap until it snaps. Until you enumerate what you already do, it is invisible — you may deliver a dozen things, but unnamed things carry no value. Sum the stack, state the total, then reveal a price that is a fraction of it. The comparison is now against your own stack, not a competitor's.

*Terminology note: "naked offer" is a summarizer's word, not Hormozi's — the term does not appear in the book. What he actually prescribes is anchor-to-core-then-stack, above. Use "core offer" when precision matters.*

**Paraphrased example.** A weight-loss stack split into seven named parts — grocery system (~$1,000), quick-cooking guide (~$600), personalized meal plan (~$500), workouts (~$699), travel blueprint (~$199), accountability system (~$1,000), eating-out system (~$349) — totaling ~$4,351 stated value, sold at $599; later versions ran longer and sold at $2,400–$5,200. Same mechanic as the classic infomercial: one knife at $38.95, then 37 more knives, sharpeners, pans and guarantees piled on until refusing feels stupid. Identical goods, more perceived value, purely from enumeration.

**Objections are component specs.** He once declined a prospect who ate out daily, then built an eating-out guide, closed her, and found a component that served everyone.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Can we name 5–7 individually priceable components of what we already deliver?
- Stack total vs. price — is the ratio at least 5:1?

## 14. Supply and scarcity

**Rule.** Selling all available supply at one price destroys future pricing power. Under-supply deliberately: cap units, not time. Fear of loss outweighs desire for gain.

*On enhancer ranking: he does not rank scarcity as "strongest." What he does claim is that these positioning forces — scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees, naming — are **often more powerful than the core offer itself**. Hold that alongside the rule in §1 that they can't rescue a weak core; both are his.*

```
10 seats @ $500   =  $5,000  and zero residual demand
 2 seats @ $5,000 = $10,000  and 8 people left wanting in
```

**The 80/20 demand rule.** He states demand for services is fractal, not linear: roughly **one fifth of prospects will pay five times the price (or more)**. Note his own example overshoots his own rule — the same ten prospects contain two willing to pay $500 vs. $5,000, which is 10x, not 5x. Take "at least 5x from the top fifth" as the floor, and let the actual ladder go higher.

The residual is the point: after skimming the top, eight people still have unsatisfied desire, they watch the buyers enjoy it, and next cycle they move faster and pay more. Marketing's real job is to raise demand then cut supply — satisfying all demand kills the next promotion. **Hormozi's Law:** the longer you delay the ask, the bigger the ask you can make.

**Three scarcity categories:** limited seats/slots · limited bonuses · never available again.

**Three service-business caps:**
1. **Total business cap** — "we only serve X clients." Run near capacity with a public waitlist, then raise capacity 10–20% at a time while raising price, squeezing out low performers.
2. **Growth-rate cap** — "X new clients per week; 3 taken, 2 left." Ground it in real bandwidth.
3. **Cohort cap** — "X per class/quarter." Payoff is choreographed onboarding.

He frames these three as near-identical mechanics with slight tweaks — pick whichever fits how your business actually meters capacity. He adds a fourth, separate tactic: sell a **very limited supply of 1-on-1 access** at the top of the ladder.

**Physical goods:** limited releases no more often than monthly; consistently sell out; announce sold-out status publicly, which doubles as social proof. His retail illustration is a chain that ships only one or two of each item per store, so every store's selection differs and nearly everything is the last one on the shelf — which lets them price well above market and convert browsing impulses into purchases.

**Honest scarcity — most defensible:** publish real capacity ("we're 81% booked this week"). True, and it implies social proof. Apply scarcity even to lead magnets.

**Paraphrased example.** A charity gala cut the number of tickets sold and raised the price from $15,000 to $25,000 a head in a year when demand was higher than ever. That change alone added about $1M before the doors opened, and the night became the charity's biggest ever: nearly $5.4M from 100 people, ~$54,000 per head. Auction items were sold as one-of-a-kind — miss it and there is no second chance — and something that wouldn't clear $10,000 elsewhere went for $100,000 in that room. The advice that drove it, from the donor who gave it: when demand increases, cut supply.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- What is actual weekly delivery capacity, and do prospects know it?
- Do we ever sell out and announce it? If we've never published a cap, what stops us from publishing the true one?

## 15. Urgency — limit time

**Rule.** Scarcity limits how many; urgency limits how long. **50–60% of sales land in the final ~3% of the window** (for a week-long campaign, roughly the last four hours).

1. **Rolling cohorts** — "start Monday or wait." Pull increases as cohort frequency decreases. For late signups, offer expedited onboarding as a bonus or reframe the wait as an advantage.
2. **Rolling seasonal** — same core service, rotating wrapper, hard end date, recurring on the calendar. Local businesses rotate faster than national advertisers.
3. **Pricing/bonus urgency** — promotions rotate every four weeks. Integrity rule: what expires is the *promotion*, not the service. Swap the variable each cycle — discount, free install, free onboarding, extra workshop. Pre-announce price increases so fence-sitters buy before it lands.
4. **Exploding opportunity** — the window itself decays (platform arbitrage, a market inefficiency, first-mover advantage). Emphasize that waiting shrinks the return.

**Implementation rule:** countdowns must be real. A timer that resets destroys credibility permanently.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Does any current offer have a real deadline, or is everything always available?
- What's the natural cohort cadence, and would halving its frequency double its pull?

## 16. Bonuses

**Rule.** Never discount — add bonuses, and present them only *after* the price has been stated.

Bonuses are the components you already assembled in Trim & Stack, held back and presented one at a time so the value stacks in the prospect's mind rather than arriving as one lump. **Combined bonus value should exceed the core offer** — psychologically that implies the core must be worth even more.

*The often-quoted "1–10 bonuses / seven-slot worksheet" count is not in the book; he never specifies a number. His only test for what becomes a bonus rather than core is the **wow factor**: pull out the distinct pieces that could almost stand alone, especially short-but-dense assets (a checklist, an infographic, a one-page map) that nobody would pay separately for but that read as valuable when given.*

1. Always offer bonuses; present after the price, never before.
2. Stack bonuses instead of discounting to close.
3. One bonus per objection — collectively they must neutralize every perceived problem.
4. Give each a distinct name with the benefit in the title.
5. For each, state: how it relates to their problem · what it is · how you built it · what it does for them · how much faster or easier it makes their life (route it through the Value Equation).
6. Prove the value — a statistic, a past client, a personal anecdote.
7. Paint a vivid picture of life after using it.
8. Attach a specific dollar price and justify it.
9. Prefer tools, checklists, templates, scripts and swipe files over more training — lower client effort means higher value.
10. Solve the problem they'll logically hit *next*, before they hit it.
11. Apply scarcity and urgency to the bonuses themselves. **These are different levers:** bonus *scarcity* is unconstrained by time ("only people in this program ever get these"; "3 tickets left to my $5,000 event"), bonus *urgency* is time-bound ("buy today and I add the $1,000 bonus"). Miss the distinction and you get a deadline where you wanted exclusivity.

*Count resolved: the book's list runs to **11** bullets, verified. Summaries that report 13 are splitting the sub-points of bullet 3.*

**Sales-floor rule.** Ask for the sale *first*, before revealing bonuses. If they buy, disclose the bonuses after they've signed — it manufactures a wow moment and reinforces the decision. If they decline, add the single bonus that matches their stated obstacle, ask "fair enough?", and re-ask. Reciprocity makes repeated declines hard. (He notes group selling follows different rules and is out of scope for the book.)

**Sources of bonuses:** your own assets (recordings, spreadsheets, checklists) · recorded workshops, webinars, expert interviews · products from non-competing businesses at zero cost, in exchange for exposure to your buyers. Escalate partner deals through four levels: (1) their product free; (2) plus pre-negotiated discounts or free periods; (3) plus affiliate commissions to you; (4) plus exclusive licenses *and* commissions. His worked pain-clinic example on a **$400** offer: free massages, ~$100 of chiropractic adjustments, ~$50 in food-brand savings, ~$150 off braces/orthotics, ~$100 of health-club value, ~$100/mo pharmacy savings — bonus value alone exceeds the $400 price, and the negotiated referral commissions throw off roughly **$350 of extra pure profit** per sale. Stack enough of these and partner value alone justifies your entire price.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- When we're losing a deal, do we cut price or add a bonus?
- Is there a named bonus mapped to each of the top five objections?

## 17. Guarantees

**Rule.** Reverse the prospect's specific biggest fear, and make the terms equal the actions that actually cause success. Changing the guarantee alone moves conversion 2–4x.

**Template — his single fill-in-the-blank frame:** *if you don't achieve [X result] in [Y time], we will [remedy]*. Every named variant below is that frame with a different remedy plugged in. "20 clients in 30 days or we refund your payment plus your ad spend" beats "20 clients guaranteed."

**Type 1 — Unconditional.** No questions asked; buy now, decide later. Strongest raw conversion, best for lower-ticket B2C — the higher the ticket and the more B2B the buyer, the more you should steer to conditional. Framing: you're asking them to make a *fully informed* decision, which can only be made from the inside, not the outside — like not buying a house without seeing inside it. **Two named variants:**
- **"No questions asked" refund** — and the remedy itself is a menu: full refund · 50% refund · refund of their spend plus ancillary costs · you pay for a competitor's program instead · their money back plus an extra sum on top. He calls this strong but unoriginal, and genuinely risky: you're liable whether or not the failure was your fault.
- **Satisfaction-based refund** — refund available any time they feel the service level fell short. This was his own weight-loss guarantee. It pairs with a best-case/worst-case close: best case you get the result, worst case you tell me I was terrible, I write the check, and you got the program free — the only guaranteed-bad option is walking out.

Adding conditions to an unconditional guarantee weakens it fast; the more conditions, the less teeth it has. Separately, **name it vividly** — this is a presentation tip, not a third variant. A generic "30-day money-back satisfaction guarantee" is dead language; a concrete, slightly absurd image ("if you wouldn't dive into shark-infested water to get this back, we refund every dollar") is what people remember.

**Type 2 — Conditional.** Terms attached; his preference for higher-ticket work. Always **better than money back**, and **match terms to the activation points of your program** — whatever someone must do to succeed becomes the condition. Ideal outcome: everyone qualifies because everyone got the result. **Ten named variants:** **outsized refund** (2–3x their money, for high-margin offers, paired with a consumption condition) · **service** (work free until the goal lands) · **modified service** (free work for ~2x the original duration, capping your liability) · **credit-based** · **personal service** (1-on-1 free until they get there) · **hotel & airfare perks** · **wage payment** (their hourly rate for the hours they spent with you, plus the refund) · **release of service** (they're let out of the commitment) · **delayed second payment** (no second bill until the first outcome lands) · **first outcome** (you cover ad spend or travel until the first result). *Corrected: the eighth is "release of service," not "release of commitment."*

**Type 3 — Anti-guarantee.** All sales final, backed by a reason: the deliverable can't be un-seen (proprietary process, scripts, source code, deep customization). Positioned as strength; filters for self-starters. Best for high-ticket, customized, consumable or copyable offerings.

**Type 4 — Implied / performance.** Paid on results: **performance** ($X per sale, per show, per pound) · **revenue share** (10–25% of top line, or of growth over baseline) · **profit share** · **ratchets** (10% above X, 20% above Y) · **triggers** at milestones. Hybrid: minimum plus performance (e.g. $1,000/mo + 10% of revenue).

**Stacking — two named forms:** (A) an unconditional on top of a conditional (30-day no-questions *plus* 90-day triple-money-back); (B) two conditionals around **different or sequential outcomes** ("$10,000 by day 60 and $30,000 by day 90, provided you do 1, 2 and 3"). Form B does double duty: it future-paces the prospect through a timeline they now believe, which raises perceived likelihood before it ever reverses risk.

**Also:** build the terms from their stated fears — ask what they don't want to happen if they pay you, and reverse that. More specific and more creative beats broader. Tie the guarantee to payment structure (e.g. offer it only on pay-in-full) to steer people toward the option you want.

```
No guarantee:    100 sales ×  5% refunds =  95 net
With guarantee:  130 sales × 10% refunds = 117 net   →  1.23x
```
Break-even requires the refund rate to rise by the same *absolute* amount as the sales lift. Emotion says a doubled refund rate is a disaster; arithmetic says it's a 23% gain.

**Paraphrased cases.** A $25,000 no-questions conditional refund produced ~$3M in extra sales against 10 refunds actually paid. His own satisfaction guarantee on weight-loss sales was taken up by **2 people out of ~4,000 sales over three and a half years**. An agency moving from retainer to performance went from ~$20K to ~$200K/month.

**When NOT to use one:**
- Never to paper over a weak product or sales team — it converts a conversion problem into a refund catastrophe.
- Not **unconditional** where per-customer fulfillment cost is high or the buyer skews non-committal. People who buy *because of* the guarantee are frequently the worst clients; conditions filter them.
- **Service-based** guarantees are dangerous for solo providers — they suit systematized delivery. **Personal service** guarantees need qualifying conditions (e.g. a spend threshold).
- Where the deliverable is consumed on contact, or a strong promise creates regulatory exposure, use the **anti-guarantee**.
- **Default starting point (his explicit advice):** start with service-based guarantees or performance partnerships. All sales stay final, so there's no refund exposure, and it forces you to commit to the customer's result. From there, either scale on that guarantee — perfectly fine — or move up to less restrictive guarantees to buy volume.

A guarantee doesn't make people buy; it removes the reason not to. His own framing: guarantees are enhancers, they can make an offer magnetic but they cannot make a business. He also notes experienced marketers spend as much time crafting the guarantee as the deliverables — it is not a footnote on the proposal.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- What's the single biggest fear stopping prospects, and does the guarantee name it specifically?
- Do the terms match the actions that actually produce results?

## 18. Naming (MAGIC) and the refresh order

**Rule.** Build the name from five slots, using three to five. Offers wear out because the market tires of the wrapper, not because the offer got worse.

| Slot | Component | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **M** | Magnetic reason why | Free, 88% Off, Grand Opening, Back to School, Inventory Clearance |
| **A** | Announce the avatar (discriminate; hyper-local wins) | Busy Moms, Dentists, Salon Owners — the suburb, not the metro |
| **G** | Give them a goal (specific dream outcome) | Lean, Toned, Pain-Free, Double Your Profit |
| **I** | Indicate a time interval (omit if compliance is an issue) | 7-Day, 21-Day, 6-Week, 42 Days |
| **C** | Complete with a container word | Challenge, Blueprint, Bootcamp, Intensive, Incubator, Masterclass, Accelerator, Sprint, Deep Dive, Reset, System, Transformation, Fast Track, Liftoff |

*Verified: he says three to five slots is typical, all five is fine if it fits, but the name usually gets too long. Balance brevity against specificity, write the candidates out, and test — that's the only real arbiter. He maps the letters to marketing functions: Magnet = attention, Avatar = discrimination, Goal = purpose, Interval = timeline, Container = method.*

Practical rules he adds: the "reason why" in the M slot can be almost anything — his own illustration is a fraternity throwing a party because someone got their wisdom teeth out; the reason only has to exist, not be good. Drop the **I** slot where a stated timeframe creates a compliance problem. Name every bonus and stacked component with the same formula, using *smaller* time intervals for bonuses than for the core offer. **Rhyme** is his first choice for stickiness; **alliteration** is the alternative — and his stated rule is not to force either. Run two to three of your best names in live campaigns rather than picking on instinct.

**Offer fatigue — change in reverse order of difficulty. Cheapest first; never touch operations until you must.**

```
1. Creative (images/video in the ad)
2. Body copy
3. Headline — the wrapper / the name
4. Duration of the offer (6 weeks → 28 days)
5. The enhancer (the free or discounted component)
6. Monetization structure — price points and offer sequence   ← last resort
```
*Verified as six steps. In his surrounding prose he inserts **seasonality** between step 3 and step 4: change the look and feel first, then the season, then the duration. Once an offer is monetized, he says rarely change it at all — rinse and repeat; entrepreneurial appetite for change is what creates operational drag. The lower you go on the list, the more operationally expensive the change, so exhaust the light options first.*

**Fatigue math.** Reaching 1,000 people costs roughly $20 on most platforms, so saturating a 200,000-person area once costs about $10,000. **Local markets fatigue far faster than national ones** — properly cycled creative makes an offer last years rather than months.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Does the name contain an avatar, a goal, and a time interval — or is it the company name plus a service noun?
- When response drops, are we jumping to step 6 when step 1 would fix it?

## What he says most businesses get wrong

1. **They price last.** Set the premium price first, then engineer value up to it.
2. **They compete slightly under the market average.** There is no strategic benefit to being cheapest; there is one to being most expensive.
3. **They fix a market problem with sales training.** A bad crowd cannot be closed.
4. **They niche-hop after one failed offer.** His standard is a hundred offers, not three — the market is rarely the thing that failed.
5. **They discount to close.** Discounting teaches the market the price is negotiable.
6. **They over-supply.** Serving everyone who wants to buy destroys the next promotion.
7. **They solve marketing problems logically.** The leverage left is psychological — perceived wait, effort, certainty.
8. **They optimize for easy fulfillment.** Easy-to-sell is hard to fulfill and vice versa.
9. **They sell one undifferentiated thing.** Decompose it into named, individually-priced parts.
10. **They add trainings as bonuses.** Tools and checklists win — the equation punishes client time and effort.
11. **They treat a guarantee as free upside.** Buyers who purchase *because of* it often won't do the work.
12. **They refuse the unscalable thing.** Create flow, monetize flow, then add friction.
13. **They change the offer when it fatigues.** Change the wrapper, cheapest change first.
14. **They enumerate five problems instead of fifty.** Exhaustiveness is the moat.
15. **They think they hit a market ceiling.** Below $10M it's almost always an execution ceiling.
