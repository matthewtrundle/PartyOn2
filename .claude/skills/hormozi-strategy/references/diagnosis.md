# Hormozi's Business-Diagnosis Meta-Framework

**What this file is for.** The diagnosis layer: how to find what is actually wrong with an existing business before proposing any fix. Load it before recommending tactics, budgets, hires, offers or price changes. Everything is restated in plain language as decision rules. Where two research passes disagreed, the disagreement is preserved inline as `(sources differ: …)` — prefer the Hormozi-sourced reading, flag the alternative.

# PART 1 — DIAGNOSE

## 1. The Constraint (master frame)

**Rule:** Output is capped by the weakest link. Improving anything else produces zero growth. Work the constraint; let the other fires burn.

- Moving something broken to adequate beats moving something good to excellent. The win is 0→20, not 97→99.
- Fix it and the constraint *moves*. Re-diagnose. A loop, not a one-time audit.
- The constraint usually sits outside the owner's competence — which is why it went unfixed. Owners work on what they enjoy and call it working on the business.
- Decision tree, not checklist: descend one branch, stop, act.
- Diagnose before prescribing. Pull volume, AOV, margin, mix, CAC, retention first.

**Framing question:** why can't you do more of what already works? The first honest answer is the constraint.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- If this improved 50%, how much more profit exists in 90 days? Not obvious and large → not the constraint.
- Which system, moved from bad to adequate, unlocks the most?
- What am I working on because I like it rather than because it binds?

## 2. Structural screen — is this scalable at all

**Rule:** No tactical excellence overcomes bad structure. Score five continuums first.

1. **Sticky** — retention. Ideal: net *revenue* retention >100% (track revenue, not logos). Churn danger: >20% in the first 30 days, ~10% at month 3, another hurdle at month 6.
2. **Expensive** — high gross margin. A $20M business at 50% margin equals a $100M business at 10%.
3. **Expansion** — market growing 20%+/yr; avoid shrinking ~6%+/yr.
4. **Air** — low complexity and capex, minimal incremental cost per customer. Counter-example: ~$100K upfront, 3-year payback.
5. **Unique** — real barriers. None → competitors flood in → prices fall.

**Pricing dead zone:** ~$1,500–$3,000/mo tends to yield 4–6 months of retention; low-touch $300–$500/mo reaches 30–40 months (~$299 ≈ 38 months). **Short retention is usually a pricing/positioning failure, not weak demand.** Premium-effort delivery at mid-market pricing is unsustainable — go cheap-and-automated or premium-and-sophisticated.

*(Sources differ: one pass treats "not scalable" as a real verdict; the other reports him calling it a misdiagnosis of impatience. Use the screen to find the weak dimension, not to justify quitting.)*

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Which of the five am I weakest on, and is it fixable or structural?
- Where does churn start — 30 days, month 3, month 6?
- Am I delivering premium-effort service at mid-market prices?

## 3. Stage locate — the constraint your size names

**Rule:** Each stage has one constraint. Working a later stage's problem is off-constraint by definition.

| # | Stage | Role | Band | Constraint | Graduate by |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Improvise | Researcher | — | Nothing is happening | Get people to try it free |
| 1 | Monetize | Starter | 1 | No money coming in | First sale |
| 2 | Advertise | Doer | 1 | New customers inconsistent | Get more people to know you |
| 3 | Stabilize | Trainer | 1–4 | Too much work for one person | Get help |
| 4 | Prioritize | Manager | 5–9 | Being everything to everyone | Niche to your best customers |
| 5 | Productize | Director | 10–19 | Low revenue/customer; weak LTV:CAC | Sell a second, premium product |
| 6 | Optimize | Leader | 20–49 | Everything is inefficient | Do better rather than more |
| 7 | Categorize | Executive | 50–99 | Every system overwhelmed | Triage |
| 8 | Specialize | — | 100–249 | — | — |
| 9 | Capitalize | — | 250–500 | — | — |

Stages 8 and 9 are named in every public listing of the roadmap but their role, constraint and graduation criteria sit inside Acquisition.com's gated playbooks; third-party reconstructions circulating online disagree with each other on the bands and read as inferred rather than sourced, so the rows are left blank on purpose. Directionally the names carry the idea — Specialize is being best in the world at one thing, Capitalize is optimizing the business as an asset rather than an operation — but do not quote constraints for these two.

Revenue tracks the same bands: 0–2 ≈ $0–1M, 3 ≈ $1–4M, 4 ≈ $5–9M, 5 ≈ $10–19M, 6 ≈ $20–49M, 7 ≈ $50–99M. *(Sources differ on headcount vs. revenue-in-$M; treat as both — designed to coincide.)*

**Stage rules to lift for a small business:**
- **Stage 2:** Rule of 100. Do *not* diversify channels — find the one that works, double down. Fix product problems in deliberately unscalable ways (driving a forgotten item to a customer at 2am is the archetype). Be valuable before efficient.
- **Stage 3:** Fix the single biggest complaint, not all of them. Cancel accumulated software. Add content/email because colder leads need time. Define "good lead" and stop talking to the rest.
- **Stage 4:** Pick the ideal customer, rebuild the product for them, raise price to match, refuse bad-fit customers. Measure lead→sale conversion, speed-to-lead, satisfaction.
- **Stage 5:** Weak LTV:CAC. Fix with a second, higher-priced product for existing customers who otherwise finish and churn.

Alternate cut: $0–1M validate the offer; $1M–3M focus plus marketing volume; $3M–10M people systems; $10M–100M functional experts. **$1M–3M is the swamp** — outgrown the owner, doesn't yet fund safe hiring. Prioritize profitability over expansion there.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- What stage am I in, and am I working *that* stage's constraint?
- Am I trying to Productize before I've Prioritized?
- What's the #1 customer complaint, and who owns it?

## 4. Supply vs. demand — the 60-second triage

**Rule:** Constrained by supply or demand, not both. The fixes are opposite; the wrong one damages the business.

**Test:** double ad spend tomorrow — sales roughly double (**demand-constrained**) or delivery melts down (**supply-constrained**)?

**Supply-constrained, in order:** (1) raise prices 50–100% — losing some customers is the point, it rations demand to capacity while holding profit; (2) change the service-to-delivery ratio — extend hours, one-to-one → one-to-many, restructure production; (3) add people last, because it's slowest and needs a training system you probably lack.

**Demand-constrained, in order:** (1) move spend from product development into acquisition — especially if churn is already low, since low churn plus low volume is a pure marketing problem; (2) raise marketing to 20–30% of revenue vs. the ~5% underspenders default to; (3) improve funnel conversion and sell more to existing customers before opening a new channel.

**Masking rule:** supply constraints disguise themselves as product problems, so owners fund product work when they needed capacity.

**Supply-constrained tells:** owner pauses marketing "until we fix systems"; delivery runs past what was promised; the team reports overwhelm before revenue actually grows; quality degrades as volume rises.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- If I doubled spend Monday, what breaks first, and who tells me?
- Am I pausing marketing? Then I've already diagnosed myself.
- Am I at ~5% of revenue on marketing where competitors run 20–30%?

## 5. Four systems sweep — where the symptom lives

**Rule:** Ask one question per system, in order. The first "no" is the constraint; ignore the other three until it clears.

1. **Leads** — enough people to sell to?
2. **Sales** — converting the prospects you already have?
3. **Delivery** — do customers stay, get results, buy again?
4. **Profit** — does the bank account reflect the work?

Playbook split (4 systems × 3): Leads = scaling ads / brand and pricing power / content. Sales = nurture and show rates / closing frameworks / proof and urgency. Delivery = value multiplication / cost and efficiency / retention. Profit = emergency cash / pricing optimization / price-raise campaigns. **Signal in the last row: when Profit binds, the levers are price and cash timing, not volume.**

**Four systems = symptom bucket; 6 M's = root-cause tree.** Bucket first, then root-cause.

**Paraphrased cases.** An appointment-setting firm was sure it needed more customers; the real constraint was the retention of who it was already signing — more leads would have accelerated churn. An HVAC-focused agency read its problem as lead generation when clients simply couldn't execute what was delivered: a delivery constraint in a demand costume.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Which returns the first "no" — provable with a number, not a feeling?
- If leads doubled tomorrow, would revenue double or would something snap?

## 6. The 6 M's — root-causing a stuck system

**Rule:** Run "why can't we do more?" through a fixed list. The first M with a real answer is the cause.

*(Sources differ materially. Pass A: Metrics, Money, Manpower, Market size, Methods, Materials. Pass B found three other enumerations, some adding Model and Manager. Only Metrics/Money/Manpower are in every version. Working spine: **Metrics → Market → Model → Money → Manpower**, with Methods/Materials as extra branches for operations- or inventory-heavy businesses.)*

- **Metrics** — if you can't retrieve the number, the constraint *is* measurement, full stop. Metrics are off-ramps: they say where and when to intervene. Cash metrics first (balance, inflows, outflows, access to capital). Benchmark against industry averages, never your own history alone — your history says what you did, not what's possible.
- **Market size** — almost never the real constraint. ~50,000+ local population supports most local businesses without geographic expansion. Owners reach for it because it's the one cause that absolves them.
- **Model** — rarely the constraint. Reported claim: he hasn't met a business that structurally couldn't reach $100M given patience.
- **Money** — three sub-branches: **leads** (marketing effectiveness and allocation), **sales** (conversion, the most trainable skill in business), **LTV** (pricing, retention, delivery efficiency). CAC converges across industries while LTV varies enormously, so **LTV holds the leverage**.
- **Manpower** — re-run the whole tree on hiring: tracking where good hires come from? Saturated the local talent pool? Can the model afford better talent? Could restructuring remove the role?

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Which M is binding, and would I bet money on the answer?
- Am I blaming market size above 50,000 people?
- Can I state, with a number, why I can't do 2x last month's volume?

## 7. Money model — LTGP:CAC and payback

**Rule:** Use gross profit, not revenue. Judge the ratio (worth buying?) *and* the speed (can I survive until I collect?). Never blended — a blended ratio hides the losing channel.

```
CAC         = (all sales + marketing spend) / new customers acquired
LTGP        = (total revenue / total customers) x gross margin %
            = lifetime revenue from a customer - cost to deliver it
Churn       = (starting customers - ending customers) / starting customers
Payback     = days until cumulative gross profit from a customer >= CAC

CFA rule (client-financed acquisition):
    gross profit collected by day 30  >  2 x (CAC + COGS)
    e.g. $100 CAC + $50 delivery cost -> need $300+ inside 30 days
    effect: each customer funds acquisition of the next two
```

Required ratio rises with human involvement, because scaling humans brings training drag, inconsistency and mistakes:

| Business type | Ratio |
|---|---|
| Fully automated, no human anywhere (~5% of businesses) | 3:1 |
| Some human element (salesperson, or human delivery) | 6:1 |
| Multiple human touchpoints | 9:1–12:1 |
| Aggressive scaling of a service business | 8:1+ |
| Manual outreach + 1-on-1 sales + concierge delivery | 20:1 min, 30:1 target |
| His own gym business, year one | ~100:1 |

*(Sources differ on the top rung: pass A caps at 9:1–12:1; pass B found 20:1–30:1 for high-touch. The shape is consistent. Also: "3:1 is the universal minimum" is wrong — 3:1 is the floor for the ~5% of zero-touch businesses. A human-delivered service underwrites to 6:1 minimum.)*

**Payback.** Target 30 days; under 12 months is the outer bound and treated as far too loose. **≥3:1 ratio AND ≤30-day payback = green light to spend aggressively.** Bills arrive every 30 days, so the money model must clear on the same cycle — a beautiful ratio has bankrupted plenty of companies. Derived ceiling: CAC ≤ LTGP ÷ 12.

**Asymmetry rule:** raise LTGP rather than chase lower CAC. CAC bottoms out at zero; LTGP has no ceiling.

**Offer sequence:** **Attraction** (first purchase — giveaway, decoy, buy-X-get-Y, win-your-money-back) → **Upsell** (solve the next problem immediately — menu, anchor, rollover, A/B "which not if") → **Downsell** (recover the no's with modified terms or features) → **Continuity** (recurring, incentivized by bonuses or prepay discounts). Build in three stages: break even on ads → maximize 30-day profit → layer continuity.

**Pattern to name out loud:** a business cash-negative for its first 60–90 days per customer ($99/mo against $150 CAC doesn't break even until month two) can never outspend a competitor who is cash-positive in 30.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- What is LTGP:CAC *by channel*?
- How many days until a customer returns their acquisition cost in gross profit?
- Given my human involvement, am I clearing 6:1 or 9:1 — or using a software benchmark?
- Does 30-day gross profit exceed 2 × (CAC + COGS)?

## 7A. The growth levers — the only dials that move revenue

**Rule:** Revenue is an arithmetic product, so there are only a handful of inputs. Naming which one you are actually pulling stops "we need to grow" from defaulting to "we need more leads."

His canonical enumeration (from the pricing chapter of the offers book) is **three**: get more customers, raise what each purchase is worth, get them to buy more times. Because they multiply rather than add, a modest lift on each beats a heroic lift on one — 1.3x on three levers is ~2.2x revenue, which is why he treats "double the leads" as the least imaginative answer available.

His scaling material adds a **fourth** dial and reframes the set as **traffic → conversion → price → churn**. Churn/retention is the one that is subtractive rather than multiplicative: it does not add revenue, it stops revenue leaving, and it silently sets the ceiling on the other three. *(Sources differ: three levers in the offers material, four in the scaling material. They are the same model — traffic and conversion together produce "more customers," price is "purchase value," and churn is the inverse of "buy more times." Use the four-dial version when diagnosing, because it forces you to look at the leak.)*

**How he prioritizes among them:**

1. **Price first.** It is the only lever with no incremental cost, no lag and no new capability required — the money is already on the table (§8).
2. **Conversion before traffic.** A point of close rate multiplies against all future volume and lowers effective CAC; traffic bought at a broken conversion rate just buys the same problem in bulk (§9).
3. **Churn before acquisition, when churn is the leak.** Pouring leads into a leaking bucket accelerates the loss — this is the appointment-setting case in §5. Note the ordering conflict this creates with rule 1: raising price on a business that already churns fast usually makes it churn faster, so fix the *reason* for short retention (positioning, delivery, wrong avatar) before pricing into it.
4. **More customers last**, because it is the most expensive lever and the one that surfaces every constraint in delivery at once.

**Where this connects:** the four offer types in §7 map onto the levers — attraction offers buy customers, upsells raise purchase value, downsells recover lost customers, continuity attacks frequency and churn simultaneously.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Which of the four dials have I actually moved in the last 12 months, with a number?
- If all four moved 25%, what happens to revenue? (Roughly doubles — that is the argument against single-lever thinking.)
- Is my churn number known *by cohort*, or am I inferring it from a total that new customers are hiding?

## 8. Pricing power — the first and cheapest lever

**Rule:** Price costs nothing, works instantly, drops straight to the bottom line. Evaluate before spending a dollar. **Close rate is a price signal.**

| Close rate on qualified leads | Read |
|---|---|
| 80%+ | Underpriced by 3–4x |
| 60–80% | Underpriced by 2–3x |
| 50–60% | Underpriced by 1.5–2x |
| 40–50% | Underpriced by 1.25–1.5x |
| **35–40%** | Correctly priced |
| Below 30% | Not price — sales process or targeting is broken |

*(Sources differ: a contractor adaptation widens the target zone to 30–40%. Use 35–40%.)*

- Service businesses run **80%+ gross margin** (~$500 price against ~$100 delivery cost). Below that the model can't fund CAC + overhead + profit.
- Raise price at least to beat inflation annually, unless low-cost leadership is the deliberate strategy.
- Doubling price is claimed to be ~**1.7x more profitable** than doubling customer count or frequency, since it adds no delivery or acquisition cost. *(Unverified: the 1.7x multiplier could not be traced to Hormozi source material in this pass — treat the direction as sound and the number as uncertain. The defensible version needs no multiplier: a price increase carries zero incremental COGS and zero incremental CAC, so it converts almost entirely to gross profit, which no volume increase does.)*
- Test protocol: double the price for the next 10 prospects and watch. Staged version: 10–15% per increase, 60–90 days apart.
- Three ways to push it through: announce a date and do it; anchor high then "discount" to the new price; let existing customers keep the old price if they prepay long-term.
- Higher price is a quality lever — funds better staff and fulfillment, which justifies the next increase. Discounting runs the loop in reverse toward commodity status.

**Paraphrased cases.** A portfolio CEO resisted raising prices; a 50% increase with no other change tripled profit in three months. In a separate teardown, $16 → $16.99 on one menu item produced ~6% more revenue and ~30% more profit.

**Suspend this rule when** close rate is under 30% (fix targeting and process first), or low-cost provider is the chosen strategy.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- What's our close rate, and what does the table say price should be?
- If we raised 50% and lost 20% of customers, does profit go up? Run the arithmetic.

# PART 2 — PRESCRIBE

## 9. Order of operations

**Rule:** Sequence beats effort. The same tactics in the wrong order lose money.

1. **Offer** — upstream of ads, sales, price, conversion and delivery, so fixing it improves all five at once. Most "traffic problems" are offer problems.
2. **Price and packaging** — cash with zero incremental spend.
3. **Money model** — make per-customer economics self-funding.
4. **Marketing with attribution** — deploy cash only where return is measurable.
5. **Capacity** — match delivery to the acquisition pace just created.
6. **Operational leverage** — scale without proportional cost.

**Monetize precedes advertise** (Stage 1 before Stage 2): advertising into an unproven offer buys an expensive education. Cold-start channel order: warm outreach → free content → cold outreach → paid ads.

**Conversion precedes traffic:** every point of close rate multiplies against all future traffic, costs nothing and lowers effective CAC. Traffic bought at a broken conversion rate permanently overstates CAC and makes a good business look unscalable.

Related sequence from a live teardown: **diagnose → systemize → train → delight → multiply** — never multiply (new locations, new offers) until the current unit is nailed.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Have I taken the free money (price) before spending money (ads)?
- Can I attribute revenue to channel? If not, a budget increase is gambling.
- Am I adding capacity ahead of demand, or demand ahead of capacity?

## 10. MORE / BETTER / NEW

**Rule:** Work in order; advance only when the prior option is genuinely exhausted.

1. **MORE** — try to 10x the volume of what already works. Fastest, cheapest, no creativity required. Pushing volume also *reveals the next constraint*, which is half the point.
2. **BETTER** — fix the constraint MORE exposed: page, sequence, CTA, script. Then push volume again.
3. **NEW** — new channel, placement, product or market. Only after 1 and 2 are exhausted.

**Budget expression:** 70% slight variations of proven winners, 20% adjacent variations (new hook, CTA, proof), 10% genuinely new concepts. Re-rank and reallocate weekly.

**Channel decision tree:** <10 paying customers → warm outreach only. Time but no money → cold outreach or content. Money but no time → paid ads. Both → cold plus content. Several channels, none profitable → cut to one and master it. One profitable → apply MORE/BETTER/NEW. At capacity → hire an operator for it. Multiple profitable → add lead-getters (customers, employees, agencies, affiliates).

**Contrarian core:** owners default to NEW because it feels like progress. Everything in your MORE and BETTER columns was once new — you already paid that cost, so harvest it first. **Exception:** when the existing channel is structurally capped (saturation, platform ban, regulation), NEW is forced rather than chosen.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- What works best, and what would 2x of it cost? Have I actually tried?
- Which working-but-inefficient system has the biggest gap to benchmark?
- Is this new thing necessary, or just more attractive than the boring thing that works?

## 11. Focus — the woman in the red dress

**Rule:** Focus is not choosing what to do; it is the capacity to refuse good opportunities. The better you get, the more attractive and frequent the distractions.

- The loop: uninformed optimism → problems surface → valley of despair → quit and chase the shiny thing → repeat for a decade with nothing to show.
- Longer commitment compounds informational advantage — the edge people who quit halfway never accumulate.
- Jobs's test: ask what you recently declined. Nothing declined means no focus.
- **20% switching-cost rule:** any change costs ~20% temporary efficiency while the team retrains, so **a new initiative must promise 20–40% improvement to be worth switching to at all.** That's the numeric filter on shiny objects.
- Say no when: it doesn't serve the current primary goal; the current business hasn't been won; or you'd split founder attention rather than add a leader who owns it.
- **Spreading-thin tells:** four channels, none profitable; can't name your best lead source; testing multiple channels for 6+ months.

**Paraphrased case.** He ran fitness coaching, a gym, a dental agency, a chiropractic agency and a gym-turnaround agency at once and everything stalled; cutting to gym turnarounds alone produced exponential growth. Operating version: build 100 ways to sell one product, not 100 products.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Is this 20–40% better than what it displaces, or merely new?
- Have I won the current game, or am I bored with it?
- Am I in the valley of despair right now — and is *that* what makes the new thing look good?

## 12. Cut vs. double down

**Rule:** Reversibility sets decision speed; margin and operational drag set direction.

- **Irreversible** (killing a line, exiting a market): move slowly, analyze deeply.
- **Reversible:** move fast, iterate. Most decisions are reversible and over-deliberated.
- **Survivability filter:** if being wrong would end you, it's gambling, not strategy.
- **Profit concentration is extreme:** ~20% of customers → 80% of revenue; ~4% → ~64% of profit; ~1% → ~51%. Pruning is usually a profit *increase*. Find the segment producing ~80% of profit on ~20% of effort, double down, gracefully exit the rest — including customers who drain resources.
- **Cut criteria:** lower margin, higher operational complexity, attention stolen from the higher-margin line. Also cut offer components clients don't value, and cut channels when several run and none is profitable.
- **Channel kill criteria:** 3+ months of content with zero leads; ~1% outreach response with no replies; heavy unsubscribes. **Nothing may be declared dead before 100 days.**
- **Double-down criteria:** already works, better margin, absorbs more resource without proportional cost.

**Paraphrased case.** An owner who expanded from lawn care into Christmas lights dropped lawn care entirely — the original business — because of worse margins and more operational headache. Legacy is not a reason to keep something.

*(Sources differ in emphasis: aggressive 80/20 pruning coexists with a strong anti-quitting stance. Reconciliation: prune customers, offers and channels; do not switch businesses.)*

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Is this reversible? If yes, why am I still deliberating?
- Which line has better margin *and* lower drag — and am I keeping the other from sunk cost or identity?
- If I cut this, what capacity is freed for the winner, and what's that worth?

## 13. Growth problem vs. cash problem

**Rule:** Profitable-on-paper and solvent are different conditions. A cash problem is a timing problem — fix it with terms, not volume.

- **Structural cash fixes:** collect more upfront; charge onboarding/setup fees; require months in advance (the apartment model — first and last); bundle, since bundles are less price-sensitive than à la carte; move to continuity.
- **Emergency playbook:** launch a cash-upfront, high-conversion offer to the existing warm audience. Paraphrased instance: with a processing balance frozen days before Christmas and ~$23K in the bank against a $22K commission owed, a challenge-style offer produced just over $100K in 30 days.
- **Cash traps:** inventory-heavy models where profit is perpetually consumed by restocking, leaving the owner on salary. Cash-constrained no matter how fast it appears to grow.
- **Reinvestment:** excess cash goes to enterprise value — for services, higher-quality talent and brand. Brand is preferred because it lowers CAC and raises LTV at once. But take real owner earnings *on top of* what the business needs: fewer than 1% of businesses exit for wealth-creating money. Hold an accessible emergency reserve. Wealth metric is savings rate, not income.
- **Debt:** not doctrinally opposed, but you have to pay it back. His own companies are described as built on high cash flow, no outside equity and no leverage, with pricing power doing the work debt would otherwise do — the money model *is* the financing (see the CFA rule in §7: a customer who returns 2× CAC+COGS inside 30 days is a lender you don't have to repay). Stated either/or when capacity is the constraint: borrow to hire the star, or grind out the capacity internally. For an owner-operated business already carrying debt, the implication is directional rather than numeric — the fix is to raise gross profit per customer and pull cash forward, because that services debt *and* funds acquisition from the same lever, whereas volume growth on thin margin consumes working capital and makes the debt worse.
- **Runway:** he treats cash access as a first-class metric (balance, inflows, outflows, available capital — see Metrics in §6), and insists on an accessible reserve, but **states no target months-of-runway or reserve percentage** in retrievable material. Don't invent one; use a conventional operating-expense reserve and label it as not-from-him.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Profitable per transaction but cash-poor? Fix terms, not customer count.
- How much cash is locked in inventory and receivables?
- If I stopped all marketing for 60 days, would cash improve? If yes, I'm financing growth out of working capital.

## 14. Volume negates luck

**Rule:** "Volume negates luck." Enough repetitions turn a probabilistic outcome into a near-certainty, so stamina substitutes for talent. Quality is a byproduct of quantity — you can't reach the tenth good client without pitching the ninety who weren't a fit.

- **Rule of 100:** 100 primary actions a day in exactly one channel, for 100 consecutive days. Forms: 100 warm reach-outs, 100 cold reach-outs, 100 minutes making content (publish at least daily), 100 minutes building ads, or $100/day of spend. First customers typically appear around week 3 — precisely when most people quit.
- **Scale reference:** ~450 pieces of content a week (~25–30K/yr) *(sources differ: another telling gives ~50/day — same order of magnitude)*. Ad production: 6 angles × 5 hooks = 30 ads, each in its own ad set.
- **Recruiting is a volume funnel too:** ~20 candidates per outbound sales hire (~5 screening interviews to advance 1 to the sales manager, plus ~4 final interviews per hire, turnover factored in). Ramp to full productivity: 3–6 months. Run four parallel funnels — internal comms, customer acquisition, talent acquisition, customer nurture.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- What is my 100-a-day action, and have I done it for 100 straight days or 9?
- Am I calling a channel dead before statistically meaningful volume?
- Do I run a hiring funnel with known conversion rates, or "look for someone" when desperate?

# PART 3 — BUILD VALUE

## 15. Four competitive advantages

**Rule:** Four generic ways to out-compete, all executable without a better product.

1. **Speed** — faster changes behavior and justifies premium pricing. Mechanisms: templated ads/emails/processes, niching to simplify delivery, longer availability, pre-making during peak, detailed notes for faster follow-up. **Speed-to-lead is the highest-ROI operational number here: contact within 60 seconds roughly quadruples sales** — he states the 4x directly, and the operating instruction is to call *every* lead that doesn't take the desired action, inside a minute. *(Provenance: the ~4x/391% figure originates in third-party lead-response research, not in Hormozi's own measurement; he asserts and teaches it. Workshop notes give a different shape — conversion collapsing from roughly 70% to 20% as response is delayed. Both point the same way; don't cite the exact percentage as his data.)*
2. **Reputation** — consistency. Control the variables so the experience matches the expectation every time. New businesses use unconditional guarantees to de-risk the purchase.
3. **Cost** — become the genuine low-cost provider via automation/offshoring, then market that to price-sensitive segments. The one case where the always-raise-prices rule is suspended.
4. **Ease** — remove friction. People pay to skip a line even for something free; convenience often outranks savings.

## 16. Enterprise value and the exit

**Rule:** Value comes from transferability and retained revenue. A business that requires the owner is a job.

- **Value-creation band: $3M–$10M EBITDA.** The load-bearing idea is that valuation is **non-linear** — enterprise value grows faster than EBITDA across this band, because the multiple itself expands as the business gets larger, cleaner and less founder-dependent. Sourced anchor point: ~$3M EBITDA at ~4x ≈ **$12M enterprise value**, and moving EBITDA to ~$8M does *not* simply produce ~$32M, because the multiple moves too. Below $3M EBITDA the highest-value work is getting *into* the band rather than optimizing inside it. *(Uncertain: an earlier pass recorded "EBITDA grows ~3.3x while the multiple expands ~1x → ~4x ≈ 13.2x EV." That conflicts with the anchor above, which puts 4x **at** $3M rather than at the top of the band. Use the non-linearity claim; do not quote 13.2x. Acquisition.com's stated portfolio target — roughly $1M–$10M EBITDA on $3M–$10M+ revenue — is consistent with the band being where they believe value is created.)*
- **LTV is the biggest driver of enterprise value** — CAC converges across industries, LTV doesn't.
- **Owner dependence is the discount.** Circulated test: disappear 30 days with no phone — does the business grow, plateau, or collapse? Anything but "grow" is owner dependence. *(Flagged: this formulation is third-party, not a sourced quote.)* The Stage 3→6 arc (Trainer → Manager → Director → Leader) is progressive removal of the founder from delivery.
- **Hiring:** past $3–5M revenue, recruit the exceptional person *first* rather than building it yourself and delegating later.
- **Recurring revenue is the cornerstone.** At a 10x revenue multiple, $1,200 of ARR ≈ $12,000 of enterprise value. Distinguish revenue retention from logo retention.
- **Concentration is a risk to dilute** — one dominant customer or referral source compresses value. Anything untracked is implicitly not cared about; keep a scorecard of value attractors and detractors.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- What breaks if the owner disappears for 60 days? That list is the discount.
- What share of revenue is recurring vs. transactional, and what share comes from the largest single customer or referral source?

## 17. The live-diagnosis question list

1. Current revenue, target, by when?
2. What's stopping you? (constraint in the owner's own words)
3. Customers per month, at what CAC, at what LTV, what's the ratio?
4. Pricing model and delivery frequency — what are you promising, how often must you deliver it?
5. Does reaching the target need **more people, a different process, or a different business model?** The key branch — the answer is usually *model*, and adding people or locations to a bad model multiplies the problem.

**Decision rules visible in his live answers:**
- **One priority, not three to five.** Sequential is counterintuitively faster than parallel.
- Ratios must match ambition: 2:1–3:1 LTV:CAC cannot support a $50M goal — change the model, don't open more locations.
- Over-delivery at a flat price is a pricing constraint: raise price or add tech-enabled efficiency, and stop blending two different services under one price.
- Advertise the high-margin, fast-turnaround niche; take off-brand work only for cash flow.
- Change the lead *mechanism* before assuming a lead *volume* problem — "book a call" → workshop mechanism produced roughly 5x (from ~2 sales/month to 8–10). Planning: 20–25% of registrants attend, ~33% of attendees close.
- **Seasons are finite:** a founder has maybe 3–4 entrepreneurial seasons left — the argument for finishing the current one.

**Intake discipline:** identify the constraint *from the numbers* before any conversation. Data first, opinions second.

**Weekly dashboard:** qualified leads/week · lead-to-customer conversion · gross margin % · LTGP per customer · annual retention · CAC by channel · LTGP:CAC · 30-day cash test · lead response time · calendar utilization. Target 70%+ retrievable weekly without manual work; typical starting point 20–40%.

## What he says most businesses get wrong

1. **You're working on the wrong problem.** Effort is almost never the constraint.
2. **The constraint sits outside your competence** — that's why it's still there.
3. **NEW is the worst option.** MORE and BETTER are untapped in nearly every business reaching for a new channel.
4. **A high close rate is bad news** — underpriced, possibly by 3–4x.
5. **Raise prices and lose customers on purpose** when supply-constrained.
6. **Ratio without payback speed is a trap.** Great LTV:CAC has bankrupted plenty of companies.
7. **Use gross profit, not revenue,** for lifetime value. Revenue-based LTV flatters bad businesses.
8. **Businesses die from overeating, not starvation** — too many opportunities, not too few.
9. **Solve sequentially, not in parallel.** One priority.
10. **More locations or people rarely fixes a model problem** — it multiplies it.
11. **Recruit like you market.** Talent acquisition gets its own funnel and conversion math.
12. **Market size is almost never the constraint** above ~50K people; it's the excuse that absolves the owner.
13. **Do unscalable things early.** Uneconomic service is correct at Stage 2 — be valuable before efficient.
14. **Fix one complaint, not all of them.**
15. **Quality is a byproduct of quantity,** not a precondition for starting.
16. **Everything is trainable.** Inconsistent staff output means no training system, not a bad hire.
17. **Short retention is a pricing/positioning failure,** not proof the market doesn't want it.
18. **Chase LTGP, not lower CAC.** CAC bottoms out at zero; LTGP has no ceiling.
19. **Don't sacrifice the first win for the second.** Finish the current game.
20. **Don't bet your personal outcome on an exit.** Take owner earnings on top of what the business needs.
21. **"Grow" defaults to "more leads" and shouldn't.** The levers multiply — small gains on customers, purchase value and frequency together beat a heroic gain on any one, and churn quietly caps all three.

## Run the audit in this order

1. **Structural screen** (§2) — Sticky / Expensive / Expansion / Air / Unique. Am I optimizing a capped model?
2. **Stage locate** (§3) — headcount and revenue → stage → the constraint that stage names.
3. **Doubling test** (§4) — 2x spend Monday: double or chaos? Supply or demand.
4. **Four-system sweep** (§5) — Leads → Sales → Delivery → Profit. First "no" wins.
5. **6M root cause** (§6) — Metrics → Market → Model → Money → Manpower.
6. **Money model** (§7) — LTGP:CAC by channel against the human-touch bar; payback days; CFA 2× test.
7. **Lever check** (§7A) — which of traffic / conversion / price / churn has actually moved in 12 months, and which one is the leak.
8. **Price check** (§8) — close rate vs. the pricing table; gross margin vs. 80%; date of last increase.
9. **Allocate** (§10) — MORE, then BETTER, then NEW, with the 20–40% hurdle on anything new.

11. **Cash vs. growth** (§13) — profitable but broke → fix terms, not volume.
