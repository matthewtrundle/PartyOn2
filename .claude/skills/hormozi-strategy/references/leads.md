**What this file is for.** The lead-generation layer of the Hormozi strategy skill: where customers come from, which channel to run, how hard, when to kill it, who runs it instead of you.

All frameworks are restated in plain terms as decision rules. Where the two research dumps disagreed, the note reads *(sources differ)*; Hormozi's own teaching is stated first and is the one to act on.

## 1. Lead, engaged lead, qualified lead

**Rule:** the output of advertising is engaged leads. Judge ads on that and nothing else.

- **Lead** = someone you can contact. A 10,000-name list is 10,000 leads and zero evidence anyone cares.
- **Engaged lead** = traded you something to show interest: replied, opted in, followed, booked, consumed your material.
- **Qualified lead** = engaged + has the problem + has the money.
- *(Sources differ: one community adaptation flips these rungs. Use the above.)*

Two ways to grow: more customers, or more value per customer. This layer handles only the first, along four dimensions: **more leads, better leads, cheaper leads, reliably** — several independent sources so no single channel dying is fatal.

**What you advertise — only two choices.** Every Core Four action carries either (a) the **core offer** itself, pitched directly, or (b) a **lead magnet** that earns engagement first. Direct-to-offer only works when the offer is strong enough to survive a cold audience; otherwise the ask is too big and the channel looks broken when the real fault is the size of the commitment. Paraphrased illustration: a webinar that pulled dozens of registrants and zero sales was replaced with a small free case-study deliverable, and the calendar filled. Diagnose "channel doesn't work" as "wrong-size ask" before killing the channel.

**Does not apply when** engaged leads have the problem and the money and still don't buy. That's a sales or offer problem; more traffic makes it worse.

**Diagnostic questions:** Can you state last month's engaged leads separately from list size? What share of revenue rides on your largest source, and what happens at zero? Are you optimizing volume when the leak is qualification or closing?

## 2. The Core Four

**Rule:** exactly four ways to make anyone aware you exist.

|  | One-to-one (private) | One-to-many (public) |
|---|---|---|
| **Warm** (they know you) | 1. Warm outreach | 2. Post free content |
| **Cold** (strangers) | 3. Cold outreach | 4. Paid ads |

**Adoption order.** Warm outreach always first — free, immediate, validates the offer. Then pick exactly one of the other three by the resource you have: time but no money → content or cold outreach; money but no time → paid ads; both → cold outreach plus content. Master one to profitability before adding the next; running all four badly at once is the dominant failure.

**Mastery gate before adding a channel** (derivative reading, not book text, but usable): cost per lead and conversion both predictable within ~±30%, written playbook exists, runs without you, survives 10x volume.

**Diagnostic questions:** Which single activity is profitable *and* documented? Are you adding a channel because the current one is exhausted, or because it's uncomfortable? Does the channel match your time-vs-money position?

## 3. Lead magnet framework (7 steps)

**Rule:** completely solve one narrow problem, free, so that solving it reveals the bigger problem only your paid offer fixes. A magnet that satisfies the customer and ends the relationship is worse than none.

1. **Pick the narrow problem and audience** — small enough to solve entirely free, structurally attached to the paid problem. Paraphrased: a free property valuation answers what the house is worth, which immediately creates the need for someone to sell it.
2. **Pick the solution type — three exist.** (a) *Reveal a problem*: audit, scan, inspection, test — best when delay makes it worse. (b) *Sample or trial*: best when the paid offer recurs. (c) *One step of a multi-step process*: give step one, charge for the rest.
3. **Pick the vehicle — four exist.** Software (calculators, tools), information (guides, checklists, courses), services (audits, done-for-you slices), physical goods (books, samples, kits). **3 × 4 = up to 12 magnets per problem.**
4. **Name it, then test the name** — headline first, image second, subheadline third. ~80 cents of every ad dollar rides on the headline; small word changes move preference by tens of points.
5. **Make it trivially consumable** — multi-format, multi-device, many appointment slots, fast shipping. Friction reduction alone is credited with 2x–4x lifts in take rate and consumption.
6. **Overdeliver** — the free thing should be worth more than the price of the paid thing. Signature maxim: "give away the secrets, sell the implementation."
7. **Attach a CTA with a reason to act now** — what to do in the simplest words, plus real scarcity, a real deadline, a bonus, or at minimum a stated reason (any reason beats none).

```
No magnet:   $1,000 spend → 1 booked call → 33% close → CAC $3,000
With magnet: $100 spend   → 1 booked call → 10% close → CAC $1,000
~3x cheaper acquisition on identical budget despite the worse close rate.
```

**Zero vs. tiny price.** Free maximizes engaged-lead volume; a nominal price filters for intent. **His prescription is free** — the magnet is judged on engaged leads produced, not on the revenue it recovers. What he *does* budget for is the **cost to deliver** the free magnet: independently corroborated summaries carry a worked case of a magnet costing ~$25 per lead to deliver, which lifts the campaign from roughly 3:1 to roughly 10:1 on the same ad budget. That $25 is a delivery cost, not a price charged — do not turn it into a tripwire price. *(Sources differ: one research dump read the $25 as a price and paired it with a ~$1,000 CAC; the other found no case of him prescribing any tripwire price. Circulating 40–70% paid-challenge conversion rates are a third party's community data, not his.)*

**Diagnostic questions:** Does solving the free problem *create* the paid problem or resolve it? Which of the 12 combinations have you built, and have you tested more than one name? What share of opt-ins actually consume it? Could you sell the free deliverable?

## 4. Warm outreach — the first five customers

**Rule:** contact everyone who already knows you, don't pitch, make it free, ask who else they know.

1. **Scrape the full list** — phone, every email address book, all social followings. Most people find 1,000+.
2. **Start where you have the most contacts**, work down.
3. **Personalize** — one real detail as the reason for reaching out.
4. **100 reach-outs/day**, up to 3 touches each (three consecutive days digital, weekly for mail).
5. **On reply run ACA** — **A**cknowledge by restating what they said, **C**ompliment (tie it to character, not circumstance), **A**sk a question that walks toward the offer. Loop until the offer fits naturally.
6. **Make the free offer** as a limited case study (e.g. five slots): dream outcome, proof from similar people, speed to result, minimal effort required of them.
7. **Ask for referrals, not purchases** — asking who they know removes the awkwardness because you aren't selling them.
8. **Recycle** — return to the top and work the list again.
9. **Ladder the price** — free for five, 80% off for five, 60%, 40%, +20 points per five until resistance appears. Signal to start charging: free clients begin referring people.
10. **Keep the list warm forever** with the same give-then-ask rhythm used in content, plus a periodic one-line note — the nine-word style email that asks nothing except whether they still want the outcome. It is a re-engagement device, not a pitch.

```
100 reach-outs → ~20 replies (20%) → ~4 accept free work (20%) → ~1 paid (25%)
Plan on ~1 customer per 100 reach-outs.
500/week × ~1% × $400 product ≈ $2,000/week ≈ $104K/year.
~4 hrs/day, first thing; a 1,000-contact list is exhausted in 1–2 weeks.
```

*(Sources differ downstream: A and most of B give 100 → 20 → 4 → 1; one community adaptation gives 100 → 20 → 5 interested → 2 customers. The 100 → 20 reply step is consistent everywhere.)*

**Stops applying** only when the list is genuinely exhausted — which, given the recycle step, is far later than people assume. It never scales past your personal network.

**Diagnostic questions:** Have you built the full cross-platform list, or only messaged people you expected to say yes? Are you asking them to buy, or asking who they know? Where are you on a documented price ladder, and when did you last rework the list from the top?

## 5. Post free content

**Rule:** publish maximum value per second, structure every unit as hook → retain → reward, and give until they ask.

- **Hook** — earns the attention switch (topic, headline, format); measured by what fraction start.
- **Retain** — holds it via lists (count stated up front), steps, or stories, with open loops resolved late.
- **Reward** — pays off the promise at or above what the hook implied. A reward smaller than the hook burns trust.
- Long-form is several units chained; that is the only structural difference from short-form.

**Five topic sources:** far past (life lessons tied to the offer), recent past (this week's client call), present (thinking out loud), trending (news plus your take), manufactured (do something deliberately, then document it).

**Headline levers — use at least two:** recency, relevancy, celebrity, proximity, conflict, unusual, ongoing.

**Seven content lessons:** shift from how-to to how-I (experience is unfalsifiable); repeat far past your own boredom; puddles → ponds → lakes → oceans (dominate an absurdly narrow niche before widening); content becomes reusable sales assets; free content retains paying customers, not just acquires them; attention spans aren't shorter, standards are higher; don't pre-schedule everything, manual posting keeps the feedback loop tight.

**Give:ask.** TV runs ~3.5:1, Facebook's feed ~4:1, growing platforms reward 10+ gives per ask. **The real rule is behavioral: give until they ask** — publish value publicly until people privately ask how to buy. Two ask structures: *integrated* (small ask inside every piece) and *intermittent* (value punctuated by an occasional promo). Don't ask when growth has stalled or goodwill isn't banked; the cost of an ask is goodwill, not money. *(Sources differ: 3.5:1 is an industry observation he cites, 10:1 is his prescription, give-until-they-ask is the principle, and a flat 3:1 is a summarizer's simplification.)*

**How much to give away:** all of the knowledge. Knowing what to do and being able to do it are different goods, so publishing the what is the cheapest possible ad for the how.

**Ask placement, worked.** *Integrated* suits long-form: three half-minute asks inside a 60-minute episode is ~58.5 minutes of give against 1.5 minutes of ask. *Intermittent* suits short-form: ten pure-give pieces, then an ask on the eleventh.

**Expansion — pick one:** depth then width (saturate one platform, then add), or width then depth (repurpose across all relevant platforms early, then optimize together). "Saturate" has a ceiling: roughly up to ~10 short-form pieces per day, or long-form up to ~5 days per week, before you are adding volume the platform won't reward. Track total reach *and* growth rate monthly, but manage to inputs.

**Diagnostic questions:** For your last 20 posts, can you name the hook, retention device and reward? What is your give:ask by count, and is anyone privately asking to buy? Are you withholding your best material on a cannibalization theory? Is volume 10x-able without hiring?

## 6. Cold outreach

**Rule:** cold outreach is warm outreach minus trust. Buy the trust back with personalization plus disproportionate upfront value, then brute-force the rest with volume and follow-up.

**Three problems, three fixes:**
- *No way to contact them* → build lists three ways: scraping software (test a few hundred for accuracy before scaling), brokers (sample several, test, buy the winner), manual assembly from communities and directories. Anything you can search competitors can too; hand-built lists are fresher and less burned.
- *Don't know what to say* → lead with big, fast value — something people normally pay for, landed inside ~30 seconds. Paraphrased: replacing a vague free consult with a substantially larger free deliverable roughly tripled take rate. Personalize with 1–3 findable details in the subject line or first sentence; third-grade reading level is credited with ~50% more responses.
- *Not enough respond* → volume plus persistence, with automated delivery and distribution.

**Scripts:** phone/chat 1–2 pages max, cold email under half a page. Structure: personal reference → social proof from a similar client → value → a soft commitment question about availability. Run 100 conversations or 10,000 emails *before* optimizing.

**Follow-up:** multiple touches across multiple channels; 2–3 conversations before a high-ticket close; if email fails, call and reference the email. Recycle dead lists after 3–6 months. Assign reps ~70 leads/week even when 100 is the ceiling.

```
Phone: 100 dials → 20% pickup → 25% accept  = 4 engaged (~1/hour)
Email: 100 sends → 30% open → 10% reply     = ~3 engaged (B2B high-ticket benchmark 4%)
DM:    100 personalized → 20% reply         = ~20 engaged
Rep economics: $15/hr + $50/appt set, leads $0.10, 200 leads/day/rep
  → ~2 appts/day → ~$120/appt → 33% close → $360 CAC vs $3,600 profit = 10:1
Scale case: 2,000 emails/day via one VA → 40 engaged/day → 10% close → 4 customers/day @ $20K ≈ $2.4M/mo
```

*(Verification note: a second, independent chapter-level summary reproduces all three rows — phone 20% pickup × 25% acceptance, email 30% open × 10% reply, DM ~20% reply — and the same rep economics, so the numbers are his illustration rather than a summarizer's invention. The DM row is still an order of magnitude above the other two; treat all three as worked examples of the arithmetic, not as benchmarks to hit.)*

**Seven advantages:** one polished message instead of endless creative refresh; invisible to competitors; inputs map to outputs predictably; immune to algorithm changes; lighter compliance burden; no spokesperson dependency, so the business stays sellable; hard to copy.

**Poor fit when** the buyer isn't definable enough to list, compliance is tight, or the ticket is too low for per-touch human cost.

**Diagnostic questions:** Can you define the buyer precisely enough to build a list today? What is the free deliverable, and would a stranger pay for it? How many touches before you quit, and have you recycled a dead list at 6 months?

## 7. Paid ads

**Rule:** an ad is callout → value → CTA, and the only cap on spend is unit economics.

**Four steps:** pick a platform (you've used it, can target your buyer, understand its formats, can afford the minimum) → target → craft the ad → capture permission on a landing page that mirrors the ad visually and verbally.

- **Callout (first ~3–5 seconds).** Verbal: identity labels naming the exact buyer and city, yes-questions, if-then statements, outsized result claims. Nonverbal: contrast, showing people who look like the buyer, scene-setting.
- **Value — what / who / when.** *What:* all four value levers and their inverses — dream outcome vs. nightmare, likelihood vs. risk, speed vs. delay, ease vs. effort. *Who:* the status change in the eyes of spouse, kids, colleagues, rivals. *When:* consequences across past, present, future.
- **CTA.** Explicit, frictionless, short memorable URL, layered with urgency/scarcity/bonuses.

**Targeting:** lookalikes from uploaded lists ranked by source quality — paying customers > warm contacts > cold leads — then filters (age, income, geo, interest, time). Every filter burns audience faster, and a local business is already a fraction of a percent of the country. Use copy to repel the wrong people.

**Testing volume:** 30+ callout variations × 10 main ads = 300+ combinations. Top-0.1% advertisers split-test ~11x more creative than average.

**Three spending phases.** (1) **Track money** — full conversion tracking before meaningful spend. (2) **Lose money** — budget up to **2x the cash a customer generates in their first 30 days** per new ad; **kill at 1x that figure if leads are zero**; if leads exist but are unprofitable, spend to the 2x ceiling while optimizing. Ten $100 tests where nine die and one returns $500 is a normal good outcome — then scale the winner 10–100x. (3) **Print money** — reverse-engineer from capacity: (target customers × CAC) + ~20% buffer for efficiency decay.

```
LTGP = lifetime revenue − cost of delivery      (gross profit, NOT revenue)
CAC  = all-in cost to acquire one customer
>1:1 technically profitable · <3:1 don't scale · 3:1 minimum · 5:1+ strong
Maximum allowable CAC = LTGP ÷ 3   (the 3:1 floor restated as a spending ceiling)
CAC well below industry average → stop optimizing ads, raise LTGP
CAC above ~3x industry average  → fix the ads
Cost can only fall toward zero; value can rise without limit.
Ratio says WHETHER to scale; absolute dollars say WHY.
```

> **⚠️ The 3:1 floor here is NOT universal — reconcile with `money-models.md` §4 before using it.**
> This book states 3:1 as the general minimum, but his later teaching (The Game ep. 985) makes the
> minimum depend on how much of the business is automated: **3-of-3 automated → 3:1 · 2-of-3 → 6:1 ·
> 1-of-3 → 9:1 · none → over 12:1.** A human-delivered business dividing LTGP by 3 will overstate its
> allowable CAC by 2–4× and overspend into a loss. **The automation ladder supersedes this line** for
> any business with manual sales or manual fulfilment. Every "max allowable CAC = LTGP ÷ 3" elsewhere
> in this file (§10d's payout tiers, §14's table) inherits this caveat — substitute the correct
> divisor before using those numbers.

Phase 3 sizing, worked: 100 customers/month you can actually serve × $100 CAC = $10,000, +20% decay buffer = $12,000/month ≈ $400/day. The question stops being "what can I afford to spend" and becomes "how many customers can I handle."

**Client-Financed Acquisition.** A 3:1 business can still run out of cash, because first-purchase profit arrives slower than the ad bill. **Collect enough gross profit within 30 days to cover CAC.** The 30-day window is not arbitrary — it is roughly the interest-free float on a credit card, so a business that clears CAC inside it can fund the next month's advertising with the last month's customers rather than with capital.

```
$15/mo membership, $5 to deliver → $10/mo gross profit
10-month life = $100 LTGP; $30 CAC = 3.3:1 — but 3 months to payback. Cash starves.
Add a $100 upsell at ~100% margin taken by 1 in 5 → +$20 average
30-day gross profit = $10 + $20 = $30 = CAC → payback inside 30 days, growth self-funds.
Levers: upsells, order bumps, higher upfront price — fix the money model, not the creative.
```

*(A stricter form comes from his later money-model teaching, not this book, and circulates in two shapes — 30-day gross profit ≥ 2 × CAC, and ≥ 2 × (CAC + COGS). Both are stricter than the Leads rule; neither is a $100M Leads threshold. Labor-heavy businesses need far higher ratios than automated ones.)*

**Diagnostic questions:** What is LTGP:CAC per channel on gross profit, not revenue? How much gross profit is collected in the first 30 days versus CAC? How many creative variants ran this quarter? Is high CAC an ads problem or a sales problem, and how do you know?

## 8. Rule of 100 and Open to Goal

**Rule:** 100 primary advertising actions per day, every day, for 100 consecutive days, before you may judge a channel. A primary action is the sending/posting/making — not planning or organizing.

| Channel | Daily minimum |
|---|---|
| Warm outreach | 100 personal reach-outs |
| Post free content | 100 minutes creating, ≥1 post per platform |
| Cold outreach | 100 reach-outs (automation allowed) |
| Paid ads | 100 minutes making ads **and** $100/day committed spend |

*(Sources differ on the paid leg — some say minutes, some dollars. Both: minutes is the work leg, dollars the budget leg, 100 days applies regardless.)*

**Open to Goal** is the mature upgrade: commit to an *outcome* number per day rather than an input number — five new customers, twenty booked calls — and work until it's hit, however long that takes. Some days 50 attempts, some days thousands. Rule of 100 is the input discipline, Open to Goal the output discipline; the switch is appropriate once you know your inputs-per-lead well enough that an outcome target is achievable rather than arbitrary. Mechanics that make it survivable: start the advertising block first thing, before anything else in the day, and keep meetings out of the morning so the goal can't be crowded out. Pick one of the two; doing neither is the failure.

**Diagnostic questions:** What is the literal daily input number, and who is accountable for it? Has any channel run at full volume for 100 consecutive days before being declared dead?

## 9. More, Better, New

**Rule:** three options for more leads, worked strictly in that order.

- **MORE.** Ask what stops you doing 10x what you do now, then do it. Doubling inputs roughly doubles outputs at first. Push until diminishing returns are *demonstrated*, not assumed. Paraphrased counter-example: a $2M company spending $30K/month on one platform, convinced the market was tapped, inside a $15B industry, with zero activity anywhere else.
- **BETTER.** Fix only the constraint — the step with the largest proportional drop-off.

```
Funnel: 30% opt-in → 5% apply → 50% schedule
Headroom: opt-in 3.3x · apply 20x · schedule 2x → the 5% apply step is the constraint
Why it's the constraint — add the SAME 5 percentage points to each step:
  30% → 35%  = 1.16x more leads
   5% → 10%  = 2.00x more leads   ← same effort, ~12x the payoff
  50% → 55%  = 1.10x more leads
Discipline: 1 variable per week per platform, ≥1 week each, every result logged
Cadence: launch Monday, read it the following Monday, log it, design the next test to beat the winner
Kill rule: 4 failed attempts to beat control (~1 month) → next constraint
```
Never restart from a blank slate: the test log is what makes week 30 more productive than week 3.

- **NEW**, in strict escalation: new placement on a current platform → same placement on a new platform → new placement on a new platform → an entirely new Core Four activity. Criterion: most leads for least work. Budget 3–6 months per new platform.

**Order flip:** below roughly $1M/year profit, MORE first. Above ~$1M profit, run BETTER, MORE, NEW.

**Diagnostic questions:** Which single funnel step loses the most people proportionally? How many structured tests ran last month, and are they logged? Have you 10x-ed your best channel before adding a new one?

## 10. The Four Lead Getters

**Rule:** once you personally run the Core Four profitably, get others to run it. Order: **customers → employees → agencies → affiliates.** *(Sources differ: one derivative proposes you → employees → customers → affiliates → agencies; another lists only three. There are four, in the order above.)*

**The leverage ladder — where the real jump is.** Leverage = output ÷ your hours, and it moves in four steps:

| Rung | Who runs the Core Four | Your work | Leads | Leverage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | You | High | Low | Low |
| 2 | One lead getter | Low | Low | High |
| 3 | Many lead getters | High (recruiting) | High | Higher |
| 4 | A lead getter who recruits lead getters | Low | High | Highest |

The Core Four gets run **twice**: once by you to acquire lead getters, then by the lead getters to acquire leads. Rung 3 is where most people stall — recruiting is itself a full-time Core Four job, and it stops compounding the moment you stop. Rung 4 (a super-affiliate who signs affiliates, a sales manager who hires setters) is the only rung that keeps producing while you're absent. Do the work once so it runs without you.

### 10a. Customers (referrals)

**Rule:** referred customers arrive with higher LTGP and near-zero CAC. Only two reasons you don't get them — the product isn't remarkable, or you never asked. Fix product first.

```
Referrals in − churn out = organic growth
referrals > churn → compounding growth with zero advertising
referrals = churn → you run the Core Four just to stand still
referrals < churn → treadmill
```

**Six ways to earn them:** sell to better-fit customers; underpromise and overdeliver (goodwill = value received − price paid); find what your winners did and make everyone do it; engineer faster visible wins; keep stripping effort out of the product; tell them what to buy next. Forcing standard: if you lost every customer but one and all future customers had to come from that person, how would you treat them?

**Seven ways to ask:** (1) **one-sided benefit** — pay your full CAC in cash or credit to the referrer or off the friend's price, requiring a real three-way introduction rather than a name; (2) **two-sided benefit** — split CAC ($200 becomes $100 each), strong for households; (3) **at the point of sale** — names and numbers on the contract, framed by a benefit, phrased as who else rather than whether; (4) **negotiation chip** — hold price and trade three introductions for the adjustment; (5) **referral events** — 1–4 week campaigns paid in points, credits, dollars or status; (6) **ongoing program** — mentioned constantly in content, ads and outreach so referring is the default; (7) **unlockable bonuses** — VIP status, training, premium support for referrals plus testimonials, cheaper than cash.

**Program requirements:** mutual benefit, one-click easy, unambiguous reward, real tracking, fast payout. **Threshold: 25%+ of new customers by referral marks this stage complete** (some sources 20–25%).

**Diagnostic questions:** What share of last quarter's new customers came by referral? Is the ask built into the sale in writing? Is the reward worth the social risk of the intro?

### 10b. Employees

**Rule:** hire people whose entire job is one lead-generation activity, train to process compliance rather than results, price their output per lead.

**Source them with the same Core Four** — network outreach, cold recruiting, job posts as content, promoted listings as paid ads — plus employee referrals and staffing agencies.

**The 3 Ds:** **Document** (record yourself doing it several ways, reduce to a checklist, test whether the checklist alone reproduces your result) → **Demonstrate** (walk them through it, editing the doc wherever they get confused) → **Duplicate** (they run it while you watch, repeatedly). Confusion means the step is too big or is secretly several steps; when someone fails, assume the documentation was inadequate first.

**Training and hiring:** one piece of feedback at a time; praise exact adherence even when the outcome is wrong; reward rather than punish during ramp; separate competence from performance — practice fixes performance. Entry-level hiring weights work ethic and social skill over experience; group interviews expose grit; expect high early churn; ~3 weeks to prove competence. There is no entry-level labor shortage — that's a senior-hire problem.

```
Cost per engaged lead = monthly payroll ÷ engaged leads generated
CAC = cost per engaged lead × leads required per customer
Worked case: $100,000 payroll ÷ 30,000 leads = $3.33/lead with zero ad spend
Keep LTGP:CAC ≥ 3:1.
```

*(Sources differ on the worked figures — one carries $3.33 × 10 leads = $33.30 CAC against $4,000 LTGP (120:1), the other $100/lead at 1-in-10 = $1,000 CAC. The formula is the durable part.)*

**Three limiting beliefs to kill:** do-it-right-yourself, nobody-can-replace-me, I-can-do-it-better.

**Diagnostic questions:** What is fully-loaded cost per engaged lead by employee? Is every lead-getting task a checklist someone else could run? When a rep underperforms, do you know whether it's ads or sales before firing them?

### 10c. Agencies

**Rule:** hire an agency to teach you a channel, not to own it forever.

The cycle being routed around: sign → senior rep assigned → early wins → senior rotated to newer accounts → junior takes over → decline → cancel → repeat forever, renting a capability you never acquire.

**The model, negotiated up front:** state that you want to learn the method; engage ~6 months; pay a premium for them to explain the reasoning behind each decision; run your own team in parallel on the same work; benchmark monthly; downgrade to a cheaper consulting retainer once your team matches; end it when you're teaching them. **Two-agency variant:** a good-enough agency for fundamentals plus an elite one at ~4x the price for optimization, targeting your team beating both by month six.

**Ten selection signals:** referred by someone with measurable results; recognizable clients; has a waiting list; transparent sales process with realistic timelines and no promised shortcuts; long-term strategy with named phases; clear about what they need from you; scheduled communication on multiple channels; simple trackable reporting; their own pitch hits the four value levers; expensive — price signals quality without guaranteeing it. Interview 5–10. Good agencies refer you elsewhere for work they don't do. **No money → no agency**; learn by trial and error.

**Diagnostic questions:** Is the contract structured for knowledge transfer or perpetual dependency? Can anyone internally reproduce what they do? What is your team's result versus theirs, monthly?

### 10d. Affiliates and partners

**Rule:** an affiliate is a whole business that already owns your ideal customer. Highest leverage, because cohorts layer — 10 customers this month means starting from zero next month, while 10 affiliates keep producing while you recruit 10 more.

**Finding them:** map what your customers buy and from whom, where they go and what surrounds those places, who else serves them; for B2B, who employs your buyer. Bucket into software, products, equipment, services, associations, events. **Best profiles:** your own customers, competitors' customers, adjacent businesses serving the same avatar, creators with owned audiences. Tiered structures work (super-affiliates recruit agencies, agencies recruit local businesses).

**Six-step recruitment:**
1. Build a list of **200+** candidates. Don't overthink it.
2. Make the offer about them — new revenue from customers they already have, new leads, zero fulfillment burden, fast to launch.
3. **Qualify with skin in the game** — they buy the product themselves (preferred, and having used it is non-negotiable), buy in bulk, or pay for certification priced at roughly **10–20% of average first-year affiliate earnings**. Free signup produces inert partners.
4. **Pay off your maximum allowable CAC in three tiers.**

```
On a $40 max allowable CAC (= LTGP ÷ 3):
Tier 1 —  25% = $10 → for agreeing to terms / buying the product
Tier 2 —  50% = $20 → activation: certification done, posts made, launch run
Tier 3 — 100% = $40 → sustained performance (e.g. 5+ active subs/month), resets monthly
Blended cost, worked: a 20% / 20% / 60% mix of partners across the tiers
  = .2($10) + .2($20) + .6($40) = $30 average payout, not $40
  → the channel's effective LTGP:CAC is ~4:1 even though top partners are paid the full 3:1 ceiling.
The gap between blended and max payout IS the margin. Product alternative: sell three, get yours free.
```

   *(Sources differ: circulating 20–40% commissions, 30–90 day cookies, Net-30 terms or $5–50 CPL are generic industry conventions imported by summarizers, not his numbers. Use the CAC-anchored tiers.)*
5. **Activate with a launch — whisper, tease, shout.** The shape of it: **whisper for months, tease for weeks, shout for days**, with cadence tightening the whole way. *Whisper* — starting from at least ~60 days out, behind-the-scenes curiosity with no reveal, roughly every 4–6 weeks, tightening to every 2–3 weeks once inside 30 days. *Tease* — the final ~2 weeks: reveal the product and its value elements, weekly then twice weekly. *Shout* — the final 3 days: direct asks with bonuses, twice daily minimum, escalating to roughly every 30 minutes at go-live. *(Sources differ on how far out whisper starts — one chapter-level summary says ~60 days, another says up to 6 months. The months/weeks/days proportions are consistent everywhere; treat the outer edge as a judgement call scaled to launch size.)*
6. **Integrate long-term**, easiest to hardest: they give your lead magnet away free to strengthen their own offer → they sell your lead magnet and keep the money → they sell your core offer on a **perpetual** revenue split, never one-time.

```
Affiliate acquisition cost $4,000; affiliate averages $10,000/mo × 12 months × 75% gross margin
less 40% of gross profit paid out → ~$54,000 remaining profit ≈ 12.5:1 lifetime GP per affiliate cost
Target ≥3:1, prefer 5:1+. Get 10–20 affiliates producing before scaling. 3–6 months to crack the channel.
```

**Diagnostic questions:** Who sells to your exact customer immediately before or after you? What is your maximum allowable CAC, and is commission derived from it? Do partners pay anything to join? Is the payout perpetual or one-and-done?

## 11. The one-page advertising checklist

**Rule:** if you can't fill in one page, you don't have a plan.

1. Pick the lead-getter type: yourself, customers, employees, agencies, affiliates.
2. Pick the commitment standard: Rule of 100 or Open to Goal.
3. Fill in: **Who** (you) · **What** (offer or lead magnet) · **Where** (platform) · **To whom** (audience) · **When** (first working hours of the day) · **Why** (to get X engaged leads) · **How** (which Core Four activity) · **How much/many** (100 reps, or until goal) · **How long** (100 days, or until goal). The paid version adds **how many creatives** (30+ callouts × 10 ads) and **how much budget** (learning budget, then reverse-engineered from the sales goal). Add daily cost/effort and the target it exists to hit, so the card is auditable.
4. Do it yourself until you can afford to hire it out.
5. Restart the checklist with the next lead-getter type. Repeat.

**Diagnostic questions:** Can you produce this filled-in card for every live channel? Which field is blank — and is that why the channel is drifting?

## 12. The roadmap — seven levels

| Level | State | Primary action | Trigger to advance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Friends know what you sell | Warm outreach to the full list; free/discounted case studies | Paying customers and referrals exist |
| 2 | Reliable reach at personal max | Warm outreach + daily content; run to capacity | Your capacity is maxed, the platform isn't |
| 3 | Employees scale the advertising | Hire doers — editors, media buyers, setters | Output no longer capped by your hours |
| 4 | Product good enough to be referred | Stop scaling ads; fix product, install referral mechanics | Referrals reach 25%+ of acquisition |
| 5 | Multiple platforms and methods | New audiences, placements, team, added Core Four activities | Channels run without daily founder input |
| 6 | Experienced executives own channels | Hire proven leaders, not potential | Advertising runs without you |
| 7 | Undefined (he declines to specify) | — | — |

Level triggers restated as gates, because they're the useful part: L1→L2 you have paying customers and referrals at all; L2→L3 *your* hours cap output, not the platform's; L3→L4 employees are producing leads profitably; L4→L5 referrals are 25%+ of new customers; L5→L6 channels run without your daily input; L6→L7 advertising runs without you entirely. Do not skip L4 — adding channels over a product that isn't referred is the expensive mistake the ladder exists to prevent.

*(Level 7: he declines to define it. Chapter-level derivatives gloss it as beyond-$100M / billion-dollar scale; that gloss is theirs, not his. Sources differ: one summary attaches revenue bands — $0–100K warm outreach, $100K–1M content plus first hire, $1M–10M paid ads, $10M+ affiliates. The sequence is corroborated; the dollar thresholds appear invented. Don't quote them.)*

**Scaling arc, paraphrased:** a 300-piece flyer test was too small to read; 5,000 was a valid test; the winner then ran at 5,000/day for 30 days — a ~17x gap between the naive test and the real one. Response: 0.5% acceptable, 1% a winner. Revenue stacked as channels were added: ~$400K/month → $780K → $1.2M → $1.8M → $2.5M with paid ads → plus ~$1.5M from affiliates → ~$4M/month, later $16M+/month. Realistic timeline to $100M: 5–10 years.

## 13. Kill vs. scale — the consolidated decision rules

**Rule:** there is no single kill switch. There are four clocks running at different levels, and one gate for scaling. Most arguments about "this channel doesn't work" are people applying the ad-level clock to the channel level, ~90 days early.

**The four kill clocks, smallest unit to largest:**

| Level | What you're killing | Clock | Kill when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Creative | One ad / one message | Spend up to **2x** the cash a customer generates in their first 30 days | **Zero** leads by **1x** that figure → stop. Leads but unprofitable → keep going to the 2x ceiling while optimizing |
| Test | One split test against control | 1 variable/week/platform | **4 failed attempts** (~1 month) to beat control → stop testing that constraint, move to the next one |
| Platform | A new placement or platform | **3–6 months** | Still can't produce leads at an acceptable cost after the full ramp |
| Channel | An entire Core Four activity | **Rule of 100 — 100 actions/day × 100 days**, or the Open to Goal equivalent | Only after that. Before it, "it didn't work" means "I didn't run it" |

**Volume disqualifies most kill decisions.** Paraphrased: a 300-piece flyer drop taught nothing; 5,000 was the smallest readable test; the winner then ran at 5,000/day. Running a channel at a fraction of a percent of the required volume and declaring it dead is the standard error — and the same error appears as 100 reach-outs per *week* against a rule that says per *day*. Before killing anything, compute what fraction of the prescribed volume you actually ran.

**The single scale gate — both conditions, not either:**
1. **LTGP:CAC ≥ 3:1** on gross profit, per channel. Below 3:1 you may keep running it, but you may not pour money into it.
2. **30-day gross profit ≥ CAC** (Client-Financed Acquisition). A 3:1 business that takes three months to recoup CAC starves while its spreadsheet says it's winning.

Pass both → scale by capacity, not by appetite: (customers you can serve × CAC) + ~20% for efficiency decay. Pass (1) and fail (2) → the fix is the money model (upsell, order bump, higher upfront price), not the creative. Fail (1) → do not scale; diagnose next.

**Which side is broken — ads or business model.** CAC well *below* the industry average means the ads are already good; the leverage is in raising LTGP. CAC above roughly 3x the industry average means the ads are the problem. And if engaged leads have the problem and the money and still don't buy, it is a sales or offer problem and more spend makes it worse.

**Reserve a losing budget on purpose.** Some portion of ad spend — derivative summaries cite 1%, 5% or 10% depending on maturity — stays permanently allocated to experiments that are expected to fail, because the pattern that pays is nine tests dying and one returning several times its cost, then scaling that one 10–100x. *(The specific 1/5/10% split is a derivative reading, not a stated rule; the "budget for losers" principle is his.)*

**Never kill for these reasons:** discomfort with the activity; a competitor saying the channel is saturated; a single bad month; not having beaten a control yet; or the channel not working while you also changed the offer, the audience and the creative in the same week.

**Diagnostic questions:** For the thing you want to kill — which of the four clocks applies, and has it actually run out? What fraction of the prescribed volume did you run? Does it fail the ratio, the 30-day cash test, or neither? Are you killing a creative, a test, a platform or a channel — and do you know the difference?

## 15. What he says most businesses get wrong

1. **Judging ads by revenue.** The output is engaged leads; conflating the two pulls the wrong lever.
2. **Hoarding the good material.** Give away the knowledge, sell the execution. Information isn't scarce; trust is.
3. **Making the free thing cheap.** It should be worth more than the price of the paid thing.
4. **Pitching the warm list.** Ask who they know, not for the sale.
5. **Treating free first clients as desperate.** Trade work for testimonials, then ladder price up.
6. **Believing you're over-asking.** ~1 in 5 of his daily-posting audience didn't know what he sold.
7. **Declaring the market saturated.** Almost always a volume excuse — most never 10x-ed one channel.
8. **Diversifying early.** Master one Core Four method first; running all four badly is the usual failure.
9. **Renting agencies forever.** Hire them as teachers with an exit date.
10. **Scaling ads over a mediocre product.** Referrals fail only from a weak product or no ask.
11. **Chasing cheap CAC.** Cost bottoms out at zero; value doesn't. Low CAC means the leverage is in LTGP.
12. **Trusting the ratio over the cash.** A 3:1 business can still starve; 30-day cash funds growth.
13. **One-time affiliate payouts and free signup.** Pay perpetually; make partners pay to join.
14. **Being clever instead of voluminous.** Test count beats test insight — roughly 11x more variations.
15. **Debating strategy instead of hitting the daily number.** Consistency and volume are the differentiators.
16. **Killing the wrong unit.** A dead ad is not a dead channel. Four different clocks, four different verdicts (§13).
17. **Blaming the channel for the size of the ask.** Cold audiences reject big commitments, not the platform. Shrink the ask to a lead magnet before concluding the channel is broken.

## 16. Coverage limits

Honest notes on what this file cannot give you, so nobody fills the gap by inventing something.

- **Roadmap Level 7 has no content.** He explicitly declines to define it. Chapter-level derivatives gloss it as "billion-dollar scale"; that gloss is theirs. Treat Level 6 as the top of the documented ladder.
- **No prescribed paid ("tripwire") price for a lead magnet.** His prescription is free. The circulating $25 figure is the *cost to deliver* a free magnet in a worked example, and the 40–70% paid-challenge conversion rates attached to it in some summaries are a third party's community data. If you want a paid magnet, you are off-book.
- **No employee compensation ladder.** The salary → salary+bonus → salary+commission → commission-only progression appears only in derivative playbooks, not in verifiable book text. §10b prices employee *output* (cost per engaged lead), which is the part that is his.
- **No affiliate cookie windows, payment terms or flat commission percentages.** 30–90 day cookies, Net-30, 10–40% commissions are generic affiliate-industry conventions imported by summarizers. His mechanism is CAC-anchored tiers (§10d). Set terms from your own CAC, not from those numbers.
- **Per-channel cold-outreach conversion rates are worked examples, not benchmarks.** Two independent chapter-level summaries reproduce the same phone/email/DM figures, so they are his illustration — but the DM row (~20% reply) remains an order of magnitude above the other two and no source reconciles it. Use the arithmetic; measure your own rates.
- **The stricter Client-Financed Acquisition forms are not from this book.** ≥ 2 × CAC and ≥ 2 × (CAC + COGS) both circulate and both belong to his later money-model teaching. The $100M Leads rule is 30-day gross profit ≥ CAC.
- **Revenue bands attached to roadmap levels are not his.** $0–100K / $100K–1M / $1M–10M / $10M+ appear to be summarizer-generated. The *sequence* is corroborated; the dollars are not. Don't quote them.
- **Two rules in this file are derivative interpretations, labelled where they appear:** the channel-mastery gate (±30% predictability, survives 10x) in §2, and the 1/5/10% permanent experiment budget in §13.
- **Sourcing caveat.** This file was verified against multiple independent public summaries and chapter-level derivative playbooks, not against the book text itself. Framework names, sequences and the headline thresholds (3:1, Rule of 100, 2x/1x paid-ad clocks, 25% referral gate, 25/50/100% affiliate tiers, 100→20→4→1) are corroborated by two or more independent sources. Single-source figures are flagged inline wherever they survive.
