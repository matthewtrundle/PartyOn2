# $100M Money Models — Operating Reference

**What this file is for.** Load this when deciding *what to sell, in what order, at what price, on what terms* — the offer-sequence and cash-timing layer, not page copy. Its single job: make one new customer throw off enough cash, fast enough, to pay for the next customers so growth stops depending on outside money. Every rule below is written as a decision rule with its number attached. Load-bearing numbers have been checked against the unabridged audiobook transcript, the official acquisition.com course index, and Hormozi's own podcast; where a figure could not be verified it is flagged inline and listed in §14. Prefer his spoken framing over third-party summaries — for this book especially, the summaries are frequently wrong (§15, item 7).

## 1. The Money Model

**Rule:** A money model is a deliberate sequence of offers — what you sell, in what order, at what moment, on what terms — engineered so the cash a new customer produces *early* funds acquiring the next customers. One offer is not a model.

**Mechanics**
- Map every problem the customer hits, in the order it surfaces, and have a priced solution ready the moment each becomes real to them.
- Three levers stacked in one machine: **how many** customers, **how much** each pays, **how fast** they pay. Ignore any one and the model underperforms.
- The stack is a decision tree, not a line: downsells fire on the *no* branch of every prior offer.
- The design unit is the sequence, not the product. Often it's fewer products presented more ways, not more products.

**Growth math**
```
Double customer value × double acquisition rate × double lead volume ≈ 8× growth (2³)
Triple each                                                          ≈ 27× growth (3³)
```
The levers multiply. That is the whole argument for building the sequence instead of tuning one number.

**Paraphrased example.** A rental counter advertises about $19/day. By the time the keys are handed over, the vehicle upgrade, the flexible return, the insurance and the prepaid fuel have taken the real rate to roughly $100/day — about a 5× lift, produced entirely by offering each add-on at the moment it felt necessary. Same customer, same car, five times the revenue.

**Does not apply:** genuine one-transaction-per-lifetime businesses with no adjacent problem, and businesses where delivery capacity is already the binding constraint — more demand does nothing if you can't fulfil.

**Diagnostic questions:**
1. What is the second thing this customer buys, and at what exact moment does the need become obvious to them?
2. If I mapped every dollar a customer spends in their first 30 days, how many separate offers produced it — one, or several?
3. Which of the three levers (count, size, speed) am I ignoring entirely?
4. What problem does my first product *create* that I am not selling the fix to?

## 2. The money math: CAC, Gross Profit, Payback, 30-day cash

Learn and fix these in order — CAC, then GP, then payback. CFA is what happens when payback compresses below 30 days; it is not a standalone trick.

```
CAC  = (ad spend + sales costs + marketing/sales labour + agency fees) ÷ new CUSTOMERS acquired
GP   = price collected − cost to fulfil (COGS)         GP% = GP ÷ revenue
PPD  = CAC ÷ gross profit per unit of time             ("payback period", in DAYS)
30-day cash = gross profit COLLECTED in the customer's first 30 days
```

**Rules**
- CAC is per *customer*, never per lead. Overhead (rent, salaried admin) is not in COGS; only the cost to deliver is.
- Use gross **profit**, never revenue. Revenue-based LTV flatters bad businesses by hiding delivery cost.
- Measure **cash collected**, not revenue booked. Annual contracts billed monthly fail the cash test even when lifetime value looks strong.
- **Why 30 days:** it matches business-credit-card float. Spend on acquisition, collect from customers, clear the statement before interest — you are buying customers with the bank's money and recycling the same dollar indefinitely.
- Speed dominates size. $100 spent to make $500 is a different *business* depending on whether the $500 lands in a month or over two years. Illustrative: a 1-month payback compounds to roughly 8× over three months where a 3-month payback returns about 2×.

**Paraphrased example.** $100 CAC against $50/month of gross profit: month one recovers half, month two clears it — payback lands around day 31. Same customer at $150/month of gross profit pays back in three weeks and you can buy the next customer twice as often.

**Diagnostic questions:**
1. What is my payback in *days*, and what would have to be true to halve it?
2. How many times per year does my acquisition dollar turn over?
3. Which revenue currently arriving after day 30 could legitimately be pulled inside it (deposit, prepay, bundle)?
4. Am I computing CAC per customer or accidentally per lead?

## 3. Client-Financed Acquisition (CFA)

**Rule:** Make enough gross profit from one new customer within 30 days to pay for acquiring *and* fulfilling at least two more customers.

### The three levels of client-financed acquisition (verified)

His own ladder — from the *Levels of Customer Financed Acquisition* chapter of **$100M Lost Chapters** (Nov 2025), the companion volume that carries the deep money-math cut from this book — measures 30-day gross profit against **CAC**:

```
Level 1:  30-day GP  <  CAC       → outside money required (savings, loan, credit line)
Level 2:  30-day GP  =  CAC       → you ride the ~30-day credit-card float;
                                     your credit limit IS your advertising budget
Level 3:  30-day GP  >  2 × CAC   → self-financing; customers fund the next customers
```

Level 3 is the state the whole book aims at. Gross profit has already netted out your cost to deliver, which is why the threshold is stated against CAC alone.

**On the "2 × (CAC + COGS)" version.** That formula circulates widely in third-party write-ups and matches the book's plain-English promise — enough from one customer to *get and service* at least two more inside 30 days. It is the stricter reading, because it also funds the delivery cost of the two new customers. Use it as the design target; use `30-day GP > 2 × CAC` as the threshold he actually states. Do not present "2 × (CAC + COGS)" as his formula — written that way it double-counts your own COGS, since GP already subtracted it.

```
His framing:     CAC $100 → Level 2 at $100 of 30-day GP; Level 3 above $200
Design target:   CAC $100 + COGS $50 → $300 of 30-day GP funds two more, fully served
```

**The three levers of CFA** — his own naming, from the *Three Levers of CFA* chapter. When the ratio fails, one of these three is the fix:
1. **Lower CAC** — get more customers for the same spend: cheaper channel, better offer-market fit.
2. **Increase LTGP** — make each customer worth more in gross profit, which raises your bid ceiling.
3. **Decrease PPD** — recycle the same capital faster. Payback lands the moment GP exceeds CAC.

**When it fails to apply:** genuinely long-cycle enterprise sales with front-loaded procurement, and pre-product businesses with no observed delivery cost — you cannot compute COGS you have never incurred.

**Paraphrased example.** His gym business bought leads for about $5. Roughly a quarter converted onto a ~$600 front-end program, with about $80 more in supplement profit — roughly $680 of profit per acquired customer, returning about $34 for every $1 within 48 hours. That let him open locations on about $3,000 down, every six months, without debt: the customers financed the expansion.

**Diagnostic questions:**
1. What is my actual 30-day collected gross profit per new customer vs. fully-loaded CAC + COGS? Above or below 1.0?
2. If I doubled ad spend tomorrow, what week do I run out of cash — and is that a model problem or a bank problem?
3. What single offer inserted inside the first 30 days would move this ratio most, and why isn't it there?
4. Am I measuring cash collected or revenue booked?

## 4. LTGP:CAC — the viability gate

**Rule:** Two numbers decide survival — lifetime gross profit per customer and cost to acquire one — and the *minimum acceptable ratio rises the more manual your operation is*.

```
LTGP = (total revenue ÷ customers) × gross margin
CAC  = total sales + marketing spend ÷ new customers

Automated processes (lead gen / sales / fulfilment):
  3 of 3 automated → 3:1 minimum
  2 of 3           → 6:1
  1 of 3           → 9:1
  0 of 3           → 12:1
```
Manual businesses need a fatter ratio because scaling them adds management layers, onboarding drag and infrastructure that eat the margin the ratio assumed.

**Relationship to CFA:** LTGP:CAC says whether the business is *viable*. CFA says whether it can *scale without outside money*. A business can clear 3:1 on lifetime economics and still starve because payback sits at eleven months. Both gates, not one.

*(Sourcing, now verified: this ladder is Hormozi's own, stated on **The Game** ep. 985, "The Only Two Numbers That Decide If Your Business Survives" — three of three automated is at least $3 of gross profit per $1 of CAC, two of three is 6:1, one of three is 9:1, none is "over 12 to 1" as a minimum. It is **not** a $100M Money Models chapter. Cite the podcast, not the book. Third-party claims of a "20:1 for manual businesses" rule are a paraphrase of "over 12:1", not a separate tier.)*

**Use it quarterly, not weekly.** Lifetime figures move too slowly and are too easy to inflate with assumed retention curves.

**Diagnostic questions:**
1. Which of my three core processes are genuinely automated — so which row am I actually on?
2. Is my LTGP built on observed repeat behaviour or an assumed curve?
3. If the ratio fails, is the cheaper fix raising LTGP (money model) or lowering CAC (channel/offer)?

## 5. Why a better money model beats a better ad

**Rule:** Ad performance is capped by what you can afford to pay per customer. The ad improves conversion *under* the ceiling; the money model *raises the ceiling*. Raising the ceiling is the bigger lever, and whoever can afford the highest CAC wins the auction.

**Paraphrased example (verified).** His gym business bought leads at about $5 and returned roughly $34 for every $1 inside 48 hours. At that ratio he could keep buying attention indefinitely while a competitor selling a $50/month membership had to stop and wait for last month's customers to pay them back.

> **Uncertain figure.** An earlier pass recorded a specific bid-ceiling comparison — roughly $245 affordable per lead versus a competitor's ~$53, a ~4.6× advantage. That pairing could not be located in the Money Models audiobook (parts 1–3) or in reachable Hormozi material; it may come from *$100M Leads* or a podcast. Use the *shape* of the argument, not those two numbers, in anything customer-facing.

**Decision rule:** once the model clears CFA, push as much spend through it as it will absorb. Before it clears CFA, do not scale spend — fix the sequence.

**Diagnostic questions:**
1. What is the most I can pay per customer and still be cash-positive inside 30 days?
2. Is my current CPL problem a media problem or a bid-ceiling problem?
3. What would my affordable CAC be if I added one upsell?

## 6. The Offer Stack — four roles

| Role | Job | Fires when |
|---|---|---|
| **Attraction** | Convert a stranger into a paying customer at or below acquisition cost | Front of the sequence |
| **Upsell** | Solve the next problem the purchase created; this is where most profit lives | Immediately after a yes |
| **Downsell** | Convert a no by changing terms or scope | On any rejection, any stage |
| **Continuity** | Ongoing value for ongoing payments | Last — it produces the least immediate cash, so earlier offers must pay for it |

Counts, **corrected against the unabridged audiobook and the official acquisition.com course index**: the *book* teaches **5 attraction offers, 4 upsell, 3 downsell, 3 continuity = 15 named mechanisms.** The acquisition.com course adds a sixth attraction lesson, **Free With Consumption**, that does not appear in the audiobook's attraction chapters (parts 3–4) — so 16 if you count the course. Ride-Along Apprenticeship — selling paid access to watch the work being done — sits in the *Money Models* section of the course alongside "4 Types of Offers", not among the attraction offers. Treat it as a real but separate mechanic.

These are **roles, not fixed products.** An upsell tactic can run as an attraction offer; continuity can be the thing that attracts. If you can't operate a slot yourself, fill it with a partner or affiliate product.

### Where the *other* named variants live

## 7. Attraction offers

**Rule:** Strangers understand price far more readily than claimed value. Make the first transaction feel risk-free or absurdly cheap.

**Cross-cutting rules**
- Free, deep discount and $1 are functionally interchangeable — pick the framing the market accepts.
- **Free generates more leads; discounted generates fewer leads but higher show rates.** Choose by your cost of a no-show: blocked-calendar businesses (medical, legal, trades) lean discount. His Lost Chapters data backs the show-rate half directly — people turn up for what they paid for at roughly **85–90%+**, and close rates on a discounted front end run higher, though he attributes much of that to the salesperson's own conviction rather than the buyer.
- **Raise base prices permanently before building any discount offer**, or the discount comes off a fake number and margin evaporates.
- Every free thing must terminate in a scheduled conversation where a paid offer is made. An attraction offer with no upsell attached is a donation.

### 7.1 Win Your Money Back
Customer pays full price up front and earns a full refund — pay it as **store credit**, which tested equivalent to cash — by hitting a defined bar. Qualification can be **results-based**, **action-based** (attend, log, submit photos, post a review), or both.
- About **10%** actually claim it.
- **Do not run this if your existing refund rate is above 5%** — it will expose an operational problem, not fix one.
- **Spread the credit:** a $600 win becomes $50/month off for 12 months, not three free months (free months just delay the churn).
- Past the halfway point, message them as if they've already won — kills the anxiety that causes drop-off.
- The money comes from qualified customers who stay and buy more, not from people who fail. Design for that or it becomes a refund mill.
- Best for anything requiring sustained customer effort. Wrong for passive-consumption products.

### 7.2 Free Giveaways
Advertise one high-value prize, harvest contacts plus eligibility info, award a winner, then sell every other entrant the same core offer at a discount.
- Prize anchored at highest defensible retail value. **Size the consolation discount at 10–30% *of your gross margin*, not 10–30% off the price** — this was previously recorded here as a straight 10–30% discount and is corrected. Entry window **3–7 days** from the day promotion starts, with daily countdown; non-winners get a **7-day** claim window.
- His worked shape: a prize presented at ~$5,000 of value against a ~$2,000 retail offer, consolation at ~$1,800 — he frames the gap as roughly a 64% difference between cost and value.
- **Cap entries at what you can phone inside 7 days.** Leads you can't call aren't leads.
- Two-prize variant: award a second identical prize to whoever *referred* the winner — turns every entrant into a recruiter.
- If it underperforms, the prize wasn't compelling enough. It is almost never the structure.

### 7.3 Decoy Offer
Present a stripped, guarantee-free cheap/free option beside a premium loaded with features, bonuses and a results guarantee. The contrast sells.
- **70–80% choose premium — verified.** His own tested pair: a free tier (one session a week, no guarantee) beside a ~$399 premium (unlimited sessions, one-on-one coaching, custom nutrition, results guarantee); 70–80% took the premium.
- Strip the guarantee from the decoy. Make the gap enormous. Present premium first. Use a qualifying question that forces self-selection (are you here for the free stuff or a result?). Show genuine enthusiasm for the premium.
- Optional goodwill: later surprise decoy buyers with a premium feature.
- Easy to train a sales team on; works in almost any industry.

### 7.4 Buy X, Get Y Free
Identical economics to a discount, framed as a gift — which protects perceived value. "Buy one get two free" beats "33% off."
- **Give away more than you sell:** buy 2 get 10 beats buy 10 get 2. Verified examples: a boot maker sold one $600 pair and gave two away — three pairs for $600, an effective $200 each; and a service priced at a flat $1,800 tested best as buy 6 months, get 12 free (18 months of access).
- Free items may be a different product; several cheap items often beat one expensive one.
- **Cap prepaid takers at ~10% of your customer base** so you don't hollow out recurring cash and strand yourself with unfunded delivery.
- Budget the delivery cost of the free period on day one; you must be able to deliver through the whole prepaid term.

### 7.5 Pay Less Now or Pay More Later
Offer a real choice: a discounted amount today with bonuses, or nothing today and full price billed later behind a conditional guarantee. Card on file either way.
- **Pay-now discount 20–50%** plus exclusive bonuses, presented *after* they've accepted the pay-later version.
- The promise must be a measurable yes/no outcome; make the guarantee conditional on the behaviours that actually make the product work (attendance, data submitted).
- **If more than 10% of pay-later customers cancel**, one of three things is wrong: the promise is too aggressive, the conditions too lax, or the price too high. If too many choose pay-later, deepen the pay-now discount.
- Paraphrased example: a speed-reading program offered $0 now / ~$297 if results are hit, versus ~$97 today with the recordings thrown in. Measured reading speed before and after makes the outcome unambiguous.

### 7.6 Free With Consumption *(course-only)*
> **Status corrected.** This is a lesson in the acquisition.com $100M Money Models course, filed under Attraction Offers. It is **not** in the unabridged audiobook's attraction chapters — parts 3 and 4 cover exactly five: Win Your Money Back, Free Giveaways, Decoy, Buy X Get Y, Pay Less Now or Pay More Later. The course lesson is video-only and its mechanics could not be verified in Hormozi's own words. The premise (give the headline item away, earn on the consumable or usage attached to it) is inference from the title, not his teaching. **Do not present specifics as his.** If you need a sourced free-front-end variant, use *Free Presentations*, *Freemium* or *Free Pick Your Price* from $100M Lost Chapters instead.

**Attraction diagnostics:**
1. Is my first transaction priced to convert strangers or to make margin? It should be the former.
2. Does every free thing terminate in a scheduled conversation with a paid offer?
3. Have I raised base prices *before* building the discount?
4. Is my no-show cost high enough that I should run discount rather than free?

## 8. Upsell offers

**Rule:** Sell the solution to the problem your first product just created, at the moment the customer feels it. Most of the profit is supposed to live here — **the front end does not have to be profitable if the upsell carries it.**

### 8.1 Classic Upsell
Frame as dependency: the first thing doesn't work without the second (bike → helmet → lights; training → nutrition). Fire immediately after the yes, when problem-awareness peaks. Sequence highest-margin upsells first. Bundle and *name* the bundle. Exploit hyper-buying cycles (weddings, relocations, new hobbies) when the customer is already spending in-category.
- **BAMFAM** — book a meeting from a meeting; never end a session without the next one scheduled.
- Ask in the negative to invite agreement ("you don't want anything else, do you?").
- **Magnetic middle — direction corrected.** A middle option exists to make the *top* option look like the obvious buy, not to pull people up from the bottom. His example is cinema popcorn at roughly $5 / $8 / $9: the medium is deliberately poor value, so the large reads as a bargain and most people take it. If your middle tier is genuinely attractive, it is doing the wrong job.
- **Margin ladder, corrected to ~13×.** His fast-food walkthrough: the burger alone contributes about $0.25 of profit; fries add ~$0.75, making it a meal adds ~$1.75, upsizing adds ~$2.75 — about **$3.25 on the same transaction, roughly 13× the burger alone.** (Recorded here previously as ~12×.)
- Protection and warranty upsells: an art studio charged an extra **10%** of the order for damage replacement and about **30%** of customers took it, at effectively pure margin. *(The wider "warranty upsells add 5–50% to price" band could not be sourced to him — treat as a general planning range, not his number.)*

### 8.2 Menu Upsell
Replace "do you want more?" with a guided prescription so the customer chooses *which*, never *whether*. Four components in order:
1. **Unsell** — visibly cross off what they don't need. Buys credibility.
2. **Prescribe** — state exactly what to take, how much, when, and how it works with what they bought. Describe use as though the purchase already happened.
3. **A/B** — always two options, never yes/no. Applies to quantity, start date, flavour, time slot, format, delivery speed, staff member, channel.
4. **Card on file** — assume the close by referencing the payment method they already gave you.
- Paraphrased example (verified): nineteen nutrition consults run off a benefits script produced zero sales. On the twentieth he abandoned the script and asked a preference question — chocolate or vanilla — and closed the next twenty customers in a row. From then on each new challenge group reliably produced **$5,000–$10,000 a month** in supplement upsells. (Recorded here previously as "five figures a month" — the sourced range is $5–10K.)
- **Menu decoy:** price a bundle identically to a mid single option — most buyers take the bundle.
- Nudge phrasing works ("this is my favourite," "Tuesdays are quieter"). If sold out, still take payment and set an expectation.

### 8.3 Anchor Upsell
Show a premium priced **5–10× the core offer** first, sincerely. Wait for the sharp intake of breath. Ask whether the premium's differentiators actually matter to them. Then present the core as the rescue, and ask for the payment method.
- Anchor and core must share the same **primary** features; only secondary features (materials, prestige, design) differ. A fake anchor destroys credibility — and a few people genuinely buy the anchor, which is disproportionately profitable.
- No gasp means the anchor is too small.
- Paraphrased example (verified): a ~$16,000 anchor suit against a ~$2,200 core suit — about 7.3× — plus ~$300 of accessories took a customer who walked in budgeting $500 to about $2,500 spent.
- **Asymmetry argument:** pitching a $1,000 offer to a $100 budget costs you $100. Pitching $100 to a $1,000 budget costs you $900. Anchor high because the errors aren't symmetric.
- **Related, from his later teaching (ep. 986):** in a $100-vs-$1,000 split where 90 of 100 buy the cheap thing at 40% margin ($3,600) and 10 buy the premium ($10,000), the premium produces roughly three times the profit — about three-quarters of everything you make — off a tenth of the buyers. Adding a tier with a zero on the end roughly doubles revenue.
- *(Verified: 5–10× is correct. A third-party summary claiming 2–4× is wrong.)*

### 8.4 Rollover Upsell
Credit prior spend — with you *or with a competitor* — toward a larger next purchase, as a one-time, decide-now proposition.
- **Four targets:** lapsed customers (6+ months quiet), currently upset customers (credit instead of refund), a competitor's upset customers (mine their negative reviews, credit their sunk cost), and current customers.
- **Size it: the new offer must be at least 4× the credit**, capping the effective discount at 25%.
- **Direction:** always more expensive — more quantity, higher quality, or a new category. Never roll into something cheaper.
- Apply the credit fully or spread it ($600 credit → $50/month across a 12-month membership).
- Paraphrased example: one operator recorded personalised videos to roughly 200 past customers offering a $4,000 credit, got about 20% response, and reported ~$1.9M in added annual revenue from a single day of outreach.
- **Gift-card play:** sell $200 cards for $20 (limit two, giftable to others only). The recipient arrives with $200 of credit and rolls it into a $1,000+ package — you get paid for the referral, then upsell it.

**Other upsell mechanics:** give bonuses up front *because* they create the next problem; deliver every promised bonus even after the yes; let staff unsell and help customers game the system (it builds the trust the prescription needs); put the item physically in their hands before asking for the decision.

**Failure modes:** offering something they don't want; offering before problem-awareness; the customer not believing it works; treating the anchor as theatre; too small a price gap between tiers to shift perception.

**Upsell diagnostics:**
1. What problem does my core product create within 48 hours, and do I sell the fix?
2. Am I asking "would you like…" or "which of these two…"?
3. Is there any anchor above my main offer, or is my most expensive product also my first pitch?
4. Who are my lapsed and upset customers, and what credit could I hand them toward something bigger?

## 9. Downsell offers

**Governing rule:** A no rejects *this configuration*, not all offers. You may offer something **different** for less. You may never offer the **same thing** for less — sliding a $5,000 policy to $400 unchanged converts a price objection into a trust problem.

**Headline result (verified, baseline corrected to 25%).** In his worked case a single full-price option closed **25 of every 100 calls**. After adding a feature downsell — the cheaper version with the money-back guarantee stripped out — **35 bought the full-price offer and another 40 took the downsell: 75 per 100 total.** Full-price sales rose by ten points *because* people saw what removing the guarantee actually cost them. His simpler illustration of the same effect: of 10 leads he might close 3; with a downsell he closes 3 more, for 6.

**Temperature check between attempts:** after two consecutive declines, ask how badly they want it on a 1–10 scale. **8 or above → keep going. 7 or below → stop, ask what a 10 would look like**, then recombine features.

**Alternate payment-plan and feature downsells** to break resistance from two directions.

### 9.1 Payment Plan Downsells — 7 steps in order
1. **Reward paying in full** with a discount rather than showing visible interest (bake financing cost into the base price).
2. **Third-party financing** — processors, lenders, layaway.
3. **50/50** — half now, half next payday.
4. **Verify desire** — 1–10 check, need 8+.
5. **Three payments** timed to paycheck cycles.
6. **Spread evenly across the term** (a 16-week program billed as 16 weekly charges).
7. **Free trial with card on file** — last resort (§9.2).

- **Align charge dates to paydays — recovers roughly a third of otherwise-declined transactions.**
- Build a payment-plan *upsell*: mid-plan, re-offer the prepay discount.
- Watch cannibalisation: both total close rate *and* full-price share should rise.
- **Seesaw framing:** "big monthly payments or small ones?" They pick small; you then show that a bigger deposit is what makes the monthly small.
- Billing-cadence churn (ProfitWell, ~14,000 businesses): **monthly ≈10.7%, quarterly ≈5%, annual ≈2%.**

### 9.2 Free Trial With Penalty — 5 steps in order
1. **Offer it last**, only after other offers are declined.
2. **Take the card first**, before explaining terms — hold firm if they resist.
3. **Sell staying and paying** — get verbal agreement that a working trial converts.
4. **Explain the penalty rationale after the card is collected** — acceptance rises.
5. **Make check-ins mandatory**, and use them as upsell moments.

- Unlike a money-back guarantee (earn a refund), here the customer *avoids a fee* by doing the work. Still call it a free trial — precision here only creates fear.
- Reuse the exact compliance criteria from your money-back guarantee. Break the penalty per infraction ($50 per missed session) rather than one lump sum.
- Criteria examples: consumer habit program — attend all calls, post weekly, journal daily, attend the transformation call. B2B challenge — 100 outbound messages daily, report stats, attend the graduation call. SaaS onboarding — complete three onboarding sessions and activate the account by session three.
- **Post-trial branches:** loved it → longer term or higher tier; hated it → diagnose, treat as a service gap you'll personally fix, offer the better-fit higher tier; never used it → waive the fee contingent on a recovery meeting, then present a different offer.

### 9.3 Feature Downsells
Lower the price by taking something away, so the customer trades savings against loss. **Remove the highest-value item first** — that's what makes them upgrade back.
- **Quantity:** fewer sessions, shorter duration, smaller size, starter pack.
- **Quality:** lesser materials, older version, lower-prestige components.
- **Service level:** response time (next-day vs 5-minute), availability (set hours vs anytime), channel (email-only vs phone), ratio (group vs 1-on-1), who delivers (junior vs owner), modality (recorded vs live), completeness (**DIY → done-with-you → done-for-you**).
- **Guarantee/terms:** remove the money-back guarantee, shorten the warranty, add conditions.
- **Alternative product:** substitute something complementary entirely.
- Process: strip one high-value feature, drop the price modestly, ask for the close. Rejected → strip again. Two rejections → run the 1–10 check.
- Name tiers aspirationally at the top, plainly "minimum" at the floor.
- **The guarantee lesson (paraphrased):** a prospect refused the full-price offer that included a money-back guarantee. The downsell removed the guarantee at a lower price — at which point they realised what the guarantee was worth and bought the original at full price.

**Adjacent tactics:** proactively downsell current customers who aren't using what they pay for (raises retention and lifetime value); after all paid offers fail, offer a free orientation then a DIY product — roughly half of orientation attendees buy; **barter the discount** for reviews, video testimonials, social posts or referrals (the marketing is worth more to you than the discount, and the publicity is worth less to them than the money).

**Never** drop price out of in-the-moment fear. Test prices systematically, in advance.

**Downsell diagnostics:**
1. What happens right now when a prospect says no — do they enter a second offer, or leave?
2. Do I have a written ladder of features I'm willing to remove, with prices attached?
3. Am I discounting the same product, or genuinely changing what's delivered?
4. Are my charge dates aligned to when my customers actually get paid?

## 10. Continuity offers

**Rule:** Continuity converts a customer into recurring cash. Engineer the **take rate with pricing** and the **stay rate with terms.** His signature framing for why continuity matters: "You can shear sheep for a lifetime, but you can only skin it once."

### The take-rate table — the most mechanical pricing tool in the model
Set the one-time/standalone price as a multiple of the monthly rate to dial in what share of buyers choose continuity:

| Standalone price vs monthly | Share choosing continuity |
|---|---|
| 1.33× | 50% |
| 1.66× | 60% |
| 2.00× | 70% |
| 2.33× | 80% |
| 2.66× | 90% |

Underlying finding, in his words: **people pay 33% more to avoid continuity** — charge a third more for the one-time purchase and half of buyers still take it. That is why 1.33× lands at a roughly even split, and it converts a strategic wish ("we want more subscribers") into a specific price.

*(Table verified against the audiobook. Caveat on his worked examples: each row is illustrated against a $199/month membership, and the dollar figures he reads out — $399, $499, $600, $699, $799 standalone — do not divide cleanly by the stated multiples. Use the multiples; ignore the example dollars. An earlier note here called the $399-vs-$199 pairing a third-party error; it is in fact his own 50%-row example, just arithmetically loose.)*

### 10.1 Continuity Bonus
Give a bonus worth more than the first payment in exchange for starting the plan — then **advertise the bonus, not the membership.** Stack multiple bonuses, each with its own anchored value. Use things you already have (back catalogue, onboarding, setup). Keep bonuses topically adjacent or you attract the wrong customer. Price them at what you genuinely used to charge — invented values erode trust. Status titles work as bonuses.

### 10.2 Continuity Discount
Trade free time for commitment. **Four placements, chosen by whether you need conversion or retention:**
1. **Up front** — free period first, term starts after. Converts most, churns most. Best where contracts are enforceable.
2. **At the end** — earned by paying on time throughout. Converts least, churns least.
3. **Spread across the term** — 3 free months on a 12-month, $200/month deal = $50/month off. Preserves cash flow.
4. **After the first or second payment** — collect cash first, then apply.

- Delivering the discount as a physical gift card works partly because a meaningful share is never redeemed.
- **Lifetime discount play:** find where churn normally peaks (say month 4) and position a permanent lower rate to be *earned* just past it, so the reward straddles the cliff. Make it giftable to referrals.
- Set the **cancellation fee equal to the discount consumed** ($600 of free time → $600 fee). Simple to explain, simple to enforce.

### 10.3 Waived Fee
Month-to-month carries a setup fee of **3–5× the monthly rate**, waived if they commit to a term (**12 months minimum**). Leave early and the waived fee comes due; completing the term kills it permanently.
- Two acceptable outcomes: a big cash payment for flexibility, or a commitment.
- **Tuning:** larger fee → more commitments; smaller fee (1.5–3×) → more month-to-month takers and more upfront cash. Harsher terms reduce signups *and* churn; lighter terms do the reverse.
- Best for slow-proof services (SEO, investing, weight loss) where results take time.
- **If more than 5% ask to cancel early, the product is the problem, not the pricing.** Terms shape behaviour; they cannot rescue a bad service.

### Retention and cash mechanics
- **Bill every 4 weeks instead of monthly:** 13 cycles a year instead of 12 = **+8.3% revenue** at identical conversion — which at a 20% net margin is roughly a 41% profit increase.
- Add a stated **3% processing fee**; resistance is minimal.
- **Require two payment methods** (primary + backup), incentivised with a **3% discount**; push for ACH as the second — cheapest to process and survives card expiry.
- **Make cancelling easy and obvious.** It prevents chargebacks and public complaints and puts the conversation in your hands. Run exit interviews, waiving the cancellation fee in exchange for honest feedback: **saves roughly a third to a half of leavers**, and doubles as an upsell moment.
- When someone complains about a missing feature, upsell the tier that has it.
- Assign status tiers (silver/gold/diamond) at 3, 6 and 12+ months — some customers value the title over any material bonus.

### The stacking sequence, and the prepay that pays for everything
1. Profitable attraction offer.
2. Upsells and downsells.
3. Continuity (yields only a small first-month payment).
4. **Bulk prepay upsell on top of continuity** — buy 5 months get 1 free. **Only 1 in 8 customers needs to take it to raise 30-day profit by 50%.**

**Paraphrased case.** Originally, 34 of 100 signed a challenge and about half of those (17) converted to membership. Restructured: only 15 took the challenge, but 40 went straight into continuity and 8 of those 40 prepaid six months — more members *and* far more cash inside 30 days. Front-end conversion got worse while the business got dramatically better. That is exactly the trade a money model is supposed to make.

**Continuity diagnostics:**
1. What multiple is my one-time price versus my monthly — and does it match the take rate I want?
2. Am I billing monthly (≈10.7% churn) when quarterly (≈5%) or annual (≈2%) is available?
3. Is there a prepay upsell sitting on top of continuity?
4. Do I run exit interviews — and if not, how much of the saveable third-to-half am I donating?

## 11. Build order — three stages, four steps

**Rule:** One offer per stage, perfected and stabilised before adding the next. Never launch the full stack at once. **Measure in quarters, not weeks** — his own framing is that patience is the fastest route. Allow up to a year for the attraction offer alone, and expect to rebuild the whole model as the business changes.

**The four steps, in his order:** (1) pick one attraction offer that turns strangers into customers and covers its own cost; (2) pick the upsell that lifts 30-day profit above what it cost to get and serve them; (3) pick the downsell that converts the people who declined; (4) pick the continuity offer, and pull as much of its revenue as you can inside the 30-day window.

- **Stage I — Get Cash.** One attraction offer that turns strangers into customers and at minimum breaks even on ad spend.
- **Stage II — Get More Cash.** Add the upsell that solves the problem stage I created (this is what pushes 30-day profit above CAC + delivery cost), then add downsells to convert the rejections you're currently discarding.
- **Stage III — Get the Most Cash.** Layer continuity, then optimise lifetime value, then optimise profit.

**Pricing discipline while building:** launch low to maximise adoption and feedback, improve the product, then raise price in increments — and **stop raising when the revenue gained from the next increase is less than the revenue lost from the people it turns away.**

**Other rules:** verify offer legality and advertising law before launch; refund immediately on request; don't hard-sell — present the solution once the customer has recognised the problem; fill any stack slot you can't operate with a partner product.

### Worked complete models (paraphrased)

| Business | Attraction | Upsell / Downsell | Continuity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gym Launch (services)** | Decoy: free DIY materials vs a ~$16K / 16-week done-with-you build | ~$42K/year licence upsell with a ~$6K prepay discount; payment-plan downsell of ~$10K down then ~$800/week for 52 weeks | Menu continuity — ads, training and support billed weekly, roughly $100–$800/week depending on tier |
| **MicroGym (local)** | Paid fitness challenge, refundable on hitting goals | Supplement bundles, large and small; feature downsells | Rollover credit into ~$50/month off a 12-month commitment |
| **Newsletter (digital)** | Free 30-day trial → ~$3.99/month | Pay ~$2.97 now and lock that rate for life (merges attraction, downsell and continuity in one move) | Automatic renewal |
| **Dog food (physical)** | Buy 4 months get 2 free (6-month commitment) | Toy and vitamin add-ons | Auto-renew month-to-month after the bulk period |
| **Storage (27 locations)** *(unverified — see Coverage limits)* | Free first month framed at a ~$127 value | ~$47 lock, boxes and supplies | ~$10/month insurance. Customers reliably rent a unit too small — the upgrade upsell is built into the product |

Gym Launch trajectory for scale reference (verified): roughly $476K/month by month 3, $1.5M/month after the licence upsell, $2.3M/month by month 14 with menu upsells and downsells, $4.4M/month by month 20 once a supplement line was added.

Founding-story economics, for the CFA argument: leads at ~$5, roughly $680 of profit per acquired customer, about $34 back per $1 within 48 hours, locations opened on ~$3,000 down every six months with no debt; Gym Launch went on to distribute ~$43M to owners over five years before a ~$46.2M sale of a 66% stake.

**Build diagnostics:**
1. Which stage am I actually on — and am I building stage 3 while stage 1 is unproven?
2. What is my *one* attraction offer? (If the answer is a list, I don't have one.)
3. Have I raised price to the point where the next increase costs more than it gains?
4. What did my money model look like 12 months ago, and has it been rebuilt since?

## 12. Specific numbers and thresholds

**Economics**
- CFA levels (30-day GP vs CAC): L1 below 1× → outside money · L2 at 1× → credit-card float, credit limit caps ad spend · L3 above **2× CAC** → self-financing
- Stricter design target ("get and service two more"): 30-day GP ≥ 2 × (CAC + COGS) · Worked: $100 CAC + $50 COGS → $300
- LTGP:CAC minimums by automation — 3 of 3 → 3:1 · 2 of 3 → 6:1 · 1 of 3 → 9:1 · 0 of 3 → over 12:1
- 2× on each lever ≈ 8× growth; 3× ≈ 27×
- Payback ≤30 days (credit-card float); 1-month payback ≈ 8× over three months vs ≈2× for 3-month payback
- Founding economics: $5/lead → ~$680 profit/customer → ~$34 per $1 in 48h; locations opened on ~$3,000
- Bid-ceiling illustration ~$245/lead vs ~$53 — **unverified, do not quote**

**Attraction**
- Win-your-money-back: ~10% claim rate · do not run above a 5% existing refund rate · $600 credit → $50/month × 12
- Giveaways: consolation discount = **10–30% of gross margin** (not 10–30% off price) · 3–7 day entry window · 7-day claim window · ~$5,000 prize / ~$2,000 retail / ~$1,800 consolation (~64% value-to-cost gap)
- Decoy: 70–80% take the premium (free tier vs ~$399 loaded premium)
- Buy X get Y: give away more than you sell · $600 buys 3 pairs of boots · $1,800 buys 6 months + 12 free · prepay cap ~10% of customer base
- Pay-now discount 20–50% · >10% post-bill cancellation = broken offer · ~$97 now vs ~$297 later

**Upsell**
- Anchor 5–10× core (reads as 80–90% off) · ~$16,000 → ~$2,200 ≈ 7.3× (+~$300) · $500 budget → ~$2,500 spent
- Rollover: new offer ≥4× the credit (caps discount at 25%) · $200 gift cards sold at $20, limit 2 · ~200 outreach videos → ~20% response → ~$1.9M added annual revenue
- Sequencing multiple: **~13×** profit on the same transaction (fast-food ladder: $0.25 → $3.25 via +$0.75 fries, +$1.75 meal, +$2.75 upsize)
- 10%-of-order damage protection taken by ~30% (art studio) · the "warranty upsells add 5–50%" band is unsourced
- 19 consults / 0 sales → next 20 closed consecutively after switching to prescription → $5–10K/month in supplements per group

**Downsell**
- 25 → 75 per 100 total close rate; full-price buyers rose 25 → 35, plus 40 downsells · simpler shape: 3 of 10 becomes 6 of 10
- 1–10 temperature check: 8+ continue, 7 or below stop
- Paycheck-aligned billing recovers ~1/3 of declines
- Churn by cadence: monthly 10.7% · quarterly 5% · annual 2%
- ~50% of free-orientation attendees buy the DIY product

**Continuity**
- Take-rate table: 1.33×→50% · 1.66×→60% · 2.0×→70% · 2.33×→80% · 2.66×→90%
- People pay ~33% more to avoid a subscription
- 4-week billing = 13 cycles = +8.3% revenue (≈+41% profit at 20% margin)
- Setup fee 3–5× monthly (1.5–3× if you want more cash from month-to-month); 12-month minimum term
- 3% processing fee · 3% discount for a second payment method (prefer ACH)
- Exit interviews save 1/3 to 1/2 of leavers · >5% early-cancel requests = product problem
- Prepay: 1 in 8 takers = +50% 30-day profit · status tiers at 3/6/12 months

## 13. What he says most businesses get wrong

1. **They optimise the ad instead of the model.** The ad raises conversion under a fixed ceiling; the model raises the ceiling. Whoever affords the highest CAC wins the auction.
2. **They lose money acquiring customers and don't know it** — which forces marketing cuts, then debt, then failure.
3. **They sell one offer and call it a business.** One offer is not a model.
4. **They measure LTV over years instead of cash over 30 days.** Identical lifetime value, radically different business, depending on speed.
5. **They insist the front end be profitable.** It doesn't have to be, if the upsell carries it.
6. **They pitch benefits instead of prescribing.** Telling customers what they *don't* need outperforms listing what a product does.
7. **They discount the same product on a no.** Never. Change the terms or change the deliverable — identical goods at a lower price teach customers your prices are fiction.
8. **They treat a no as final.** It rejects one configuration. A downsell ladder took total close rate from 25 to 75 per 100 — a tripling — *and* raised full-price sales from 25 to 35.
9. **They make cancelling hard.** Easy cancellation buys you the exit interview, which saves a third to a half of leavers.
10. **They chase front-end conversion rate.** The restructured gym offer converted fewer people onto the challenge and produced more members and more cash. Front-end conversion is a vanity metric inside a money model.
11. **They think a great skill set is enough.** A high-level skill aimed at a low-level opportunity is a model problem, not an effort problem.
12. **They believe pricing can fix a bad product.** Above ~5% early cancellations, stop tuning terms and fix the service.
