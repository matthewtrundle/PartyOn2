## Q1 — "What are corporate event ideas that aren't lame?" · 0:14–0:46

**The opinion: the lame ones are all mandatory, seated, and scheduled at 2pm on a Tuesday. Fix those three things.**

> "Here's the pattern in every complaint: it's mandatory, everyone's seated in rows, and it's in the middle of a workday so nobody can relax. The events people actually like are optional, have something to do with your hands, and start at four-thirty so leaving early isn't a statement. Ranked by effort: an in-office happy hour with real bottles instead of warm chardonnay. A cocktail-kit build where teams make the same drink and compete. A taco-and-margarita afternoon on a patio. Renting a boat on Lake Travis, which in Austin is genuinely the trump card. Casino night if your group is competitive. Skip: trust falls, escape rooms your team already did, and anything with a mandatory microphone."

*On-screen:* the ranked list building line by line, with an "effort" meter. · *B-roll:* HAVE — cocktail kits, product shots, Austin skyline. NEED — office happy hour scene; a patio setup.

## Q2 — "How do you plan a corporate holiday party?" · 0:46–1:16

**The opinion: six steps, in this order. Most people do them out of order and pay for it.**

> "Six steps, in order. One: get the budget number and the headcount — real numbers, not a range. Two: pick the date, and pick a Thursday. Fridays in December are already gone and nobody wants a Monday. Three: book the space before you plan anything inside it — space is the constraint. Four: food, then drinks, then everything else. Five: send the invite three weeks out with a real RSVP, because your headcount is going to drop about twenty percent and you'd rather know. Six: confirm the drink order the week of, against the RSVPs you actually got. The mistake is planning the theme first. Theme is step seven and it's optional."

*On-screen:* the 6 steps as a numbered checklist ticking off. · *B-roll:* HAVE — planning/office b-roll. NEED — calendar/invite prop shot.

## Q3 — "When do you actually need to book?" · 1:16–1:38

**The opinion: venues in September, drinks the week of. Those are very different deadlines and people confuse them.**

> "If you want a real venue on a good December date, you're booking in September. By early November the decent rooms are gone and you're choosing between a hotel ballroom and your own conference room. But the drinks are a completely different clock — that's a seventy-two hour lead time with us as standard, and faster when we need to be. So: lock the room three months out, and don't let anyone tell you the alcohol has to be decided that early. It shouldn't be. Your headcount isn't real yet."

*On-screen:* "Venue: −3 months · Drinks: −72 hours". · *B-roll:* HAVE — vans, warehouse pick-and-pack. · *Evidence: published lead time "72 hours for standard orders" on the corporate lander.*

## Q4 — "What does a company holiday party cost?" · 1:38–2:04

## Q5 — "Is the company holiday party tax-deductible?" · 2:04–2:32 · **ALLAN ON CAMERA moment #2** (10 sec, then VO)

**The opinion: the employee party is the one food-and-drink category that's still fully deductible — and almost nobody plans around that.**

> **[Allan, to camera]:** "This is the one nobody tells you: a party for your employees is generally one hundred percent deductible. Client dinners aren't."
> **[VO resumes]:** "Most business meals got cut to fifty percent — but a recreational event primarily for your employees, open to the whole team rather than just executives, is treated differently and generally comes in at a hundred. Invite everyone, keep the invite, keep the itemized receipt, and code it separately from client entertainment. I'm not your accountant — ask yours before you file. ## Q6 — "How much alcohol do you need for an office party?" · 2:32–3:00

**The opinion: one drink per person per hour, and buy less liquor than you think.**

> "One drink per person per hour, plus a little cushion. Fifty people for three hours is about a hundred and seventy-five drinks. Split it forty percent wine, thirty beer, thirty spirits and that's fourteen bottles of wine, four bottles of liquor, and about fifty beers. Four bottles sounds low — it isn't. A seven-fifty gives you seventeen drinks, and at an office party a lot of people have one glass of wine and switch to sparkling water. Which brings up the thing most planners underbuy: non-alcoholic. Get two good options that aren't just soda, and get enough that they don't run out first. That's the detail people notice."

*On-screen:* "50 people × 3 hrs ≈ 175 drinks → 14 wine · 4 liquor · 50 beers". · *B-roll:* HAVE — product close-ups, N/A options. · *Math check: 50 × 3.5 = 175 drinks; wine 70/5 = 14 · spirits 53/17 = 4 · beer 53 cans. Matches the live calculator on `/corporate/holiday-party`.*

## Q7 — "What should you actually serve?" · 3:00–3:24

**The opinion: buy fewer, better bottles. Your team can tell, and it's the cheapest status signal you'll ever buy.**

> "Keep the menu short. What works over and over at Austin office parties: one light signature cocktail — a ranch water, or a spiked Arnold Palmer — a red and a white wine, and local beer to round it out. Four decisions, done. Nobody misses the twelve-bottle bar, and the difference between a forgettable bottle and a good one across a fifty-person party is maybe a hundred and fifty dollars total. Your team notices the good bottles. They definitely notice the bad ones. And in Austin, local beer reads as effort."

*On-screen:* menu card — "Ranch Water · Spiked Arnold Palmer · One Red · One White · Local Beer". · *B-roll:* HAVE — wine pours, local beer cans, product close-ups. NEED — ranch water + spiked Arnold Palmer build close-ups. · *Evidence: menu per Allan (operator, 2026-08-04) — deliberately categorical, no brand or ranking claims on camera; the n=11 top-products pull stays in the brief as background.*

## Q8 — "What about happy hours and team events the rest of the year?" · 3:24–3:44

**The opinion: a monthly Thursday happy hour beats one big December party. Different budget, better result.**

> "If you only do one event a year, it carries impossible weight. A standing Thursday happy hour once a month, same time, optional, does more for how a team actually feels than any single party. Budget-wise it's the same money spread out. The format that works best: one signature cocktail everybody's making that month, decent beer and wine alongside, and an actual end time so people can leave. And for the team-building version — cocktail kits where everyone builds the same drink at the same time genuinely works."

*On-screen:* "Monthly > annual. Same budget." · *B-roll:* HAVE — cocktail kits, happy hour scene.

## Q9 — "Do you need TABC, a bartender, or a COI for an office party?" · 3:44–4:08

**The opinion: your building will ask for the certificate of insurance. Have it before they ask.**

> "For a private company event where you're not selling drinks, you don't need your own permit. What you will get asked for — usually by your building management or the venue — is a certificate of insurance, and often proof your bartenders are certified. We're a TABC-licensed retailer, permit P-two-hundred-eighty-four-three-nine-eight, with a million dollars in general and liquor liability, and we'll send a COI on request. One thing to know up front: **we can't add your venue or your company as additionally insured** — that trips people up late, so ask your building what they need the week you book the room, not the week of the party."

*On-screen:* "TABC #P-200084398 · $1M GL + liquor liability · COI on request" then "We cannot add third parties as additionally insured." · *B-roll:* HAVE — licensing graphic, driver carding at a door. · *Evidence: corporate lander FAQ, verbatim on the additionally-insured limit. **Do not name a licensed entity on camera** — the entity name isn't documented anywhere internally.*
