# PART 2 — VOICEOVER

Read straight through at a relaxed **150 wpm**. Chapter titles are search phrases — say them out
loud as written, they're the keywords.

## Wedding VO — ~730 words, target 4:45

**Q1 — How much alcohol do you need for a wedding?**

> "One drink per guest per hour, plus a full extra hour of cushion. That's it. Guests times
> hours-plus-one. A hundred guests over five hours is six hundred drinks. That sounds like a lot —
> it isn't. It's a hundred people having one drink an hour, and it already builds in the buffer
> that every wedding blog tells you to add on top. Don't add a second buffer to a buffer. You'll be
> returning pallets."

**Q2 — How much for 100, 150, or 200 guests?**

> "Here's what six hundred drinks actually looks like on a delivery invoice for a full bar. Forty
> percent wine, thirty percent spirits, thirty percent beer — that's the split that matches what
> real Austin weddings drink. A hundred guests, five hours: forty-eight bottles of wine, eleven
> bottles of liquor, and about a hundred and eighty beers. A hundred and fifty guests: seventy-two
> wine, sixteen liquor, two-seventy beer. Two hundred guests: ninety-six wine, twenty-two liquor,
> three-sixty beer. Screenshot this one."

**Q3 — How much does an open bar cost — and is it worth it?**

> "A venue open bar runs about twenty-five to forty-five dollars a head for a standard package —
> and the top end goes a lot higher. For a hundred guests that's twenty-five hundred to forty-five
> hundred dollars, before service charge and before gratuity. What you're buying is convenience and
> somebody else's liability. What you're not buying is the alcohol at anything close to what it
> costs. The question isn't whether an open bar is nice. It's whether you know what the same drinks
> cost if you buy them yourself."

**Q4 — Open bar vs buying your own** *(picks up after the on-camera money line)*

> "Our tiers run thirteen to twenty-six dollars a head — that's thirteen hundred to twenty-six
> hundred for a hundred guests, all in. And that's not a teaser rate: price out the middle package
> for a hundred guests and five hours — champagne toast included — and it comes out just under
> eighteen hundred dollars. The venue bills twenty-five hundred to forty-five hundred for the same
> crowd. Same guests, same five hours, same drinks in their hands — the difference is that you're
> buying bottles at retail instead of renting pours at a markup. You still need bartenders — budget
> for those separately, and hire good ones. But the alcohol itself is the single easiest line item
> to cut in half without a single guest noticing."

**Q5 — What's the right liquor, wine, and beer split?**

> "Thirty percent spirits, forty percent wine, thirty percent beer for a full bar. Doing beer and
> wine only? Go fifty-five wine, forty-five beer. And here's what we see over and over at Austin
> weddings: a margarita or an old fashioned as the signature cocktail, prosecco, sauvignon blanc and
> pinot noir carrying the night, and Austin Beerworks variety packs and Shiner in the coolers.
> Weddings are wine-first. Bachelor parties are seltzer-first. Do not plan a wedding bar like a bach
> party."

**Q6 — Who pays for the alcohol at a wedding?**

> "Traditionally the bride's family covered the reception, and plenty of families still do.
> Realistically, in 2026, it's whoever is paying for the venue — often the couple. The useful
> version of this question isn't who *should* pay, it's who *decides*. Alcohol is usually five to
> ten percent of a wedding budget and it's the line everyone has an opinion about. Name the payer
> and the decision-maker in the same conversation, early, and you'll never have this fight."

**Q7 — How much champagne do you need for the toast?**

> "One toast pour per guest, five pours per bottle. A hundred guests is twenty bottles. Don't fill
> the flute — a toast pour is half a normal glass, and half your guests will take one sip and set it
> down. Buy a couple extra bottles for the photos and the second toast nobody planned, and skip the
> expensive label for the toast itself. Nobody is tasting it. Save the good bottle for the two of
> you."

**Q8 — Where do you buy wedding alcohol in bulk — and what happens to the leftovers?**

> "Buy from a licensed retailer who delivers cold, on your timeline, to the venue — not from
> wherever's cheapest by the bottle. The thing that actually decides this is the leftovers policy,
> so get it in plain language before you order. Ours is: drop the unopened cases back at our store
> the day after, and we refund a hundred percent of up to a quarter of your order — same day, no
> restocking fee. So build in a cushion, but build in a twenty percent cushion, not a fifty percent
> one, because past that quarter it's yours. That's the whole reason you can afford to over-order a
> little instead of running dry at nine-thirty. Running out at a wedding is the one mistake you
> can't fix from the parking lot."

**Q9 — Do you need a bartender, a license, or insurance?**

> "You don't need a permit to serve alcohol at your own private wedding — but the second money
> changes hands for drinks, you do, so don't 'sell' anything. What you actually need is certified
> bartenders, and most Austin venues require it in writing along with a certificate of insurance.
> We're a TABC-licensed retailer, we're insured, our drivers are TABC-certified, and we card on
> delivery because the law says so. Ask your venue for their bar requirements in writing the week you
> book — not the week of."

**Q10 — When do you order the alcohol?**

> "Book the venue a year out, the bartenders three months out, and the alcohol at least a month out.
> That's not caution — most of our wedding orders already come in thirty-plus days ahead, and those
> are the smooth ones: the exact bottles you picked, delivered in the calm week, not the chaos week.
> Don't wait on final RSVPs. Get close and round up — the return policy covers the difference. Then
> run the free calculator and order from the number it gives you."

## Corporate VO — ~610 words, target 4:10

**Q1 — What are corporate event ideas that aren't lame?**

> "Here's the pattern in every complaint: it's mandatory, everyone's seated in rows, and it's in the
> middle of a workday so nobody can relax. The events people actually like are optional, have
> something to do with your hands, and start at four-thirty so leaving early isn't a statement.
> Ranked by effort: an in-office happy hour with real bottles instead of warm chardonnay. A
> cocktail-kit build where teams make the same drink and compete. A taco-and-margarita afternoon on a
> patio. Renting a boat on Lake Travis, which in Austin is genuinely the trump card. Casino night if
> your group is competitive. Skip: trust falls, escape rooms your team already did, and anything with
> a mandatory microphone."

**Q2 — How do you plan a corporate holiday party?**

> "Six steps, in order. One: get the budget number and the headcount — real numbers, not a range.
> Two: pick the date, and pick a Thursday. Fridays in December are already gone and nobody wants a
> Monday. Three: book the space before you plan anything inside it — space is the constraint. Four:
> food, then drinks, then everything else. Five: send the invite three weeks out with a real RSVP,
> because your headcount is going to drop about twenty percent and you'd rather know. Six: confirm the
> drink order the week of, against the RSVPs you actually got. The mistake is planning the theme
> first. Theme is step seven and it's optional."

**Q3 — When do you actually need to book?**

> "If you want a real venue on a good December date, you're booking in September. By early November
> the decent rooms are gone and you're choosing between a hotel ballroom and your own conference
> room. But the drinks are a completely different clock — that's a seventy-two hour lead time with us
> as standard, and faster when we need to be. So: lock the room three months out, and don't let
> anyone tell you the alcohol has to be decided that early. It shouldn't be. Your headcount isn't
> real yet."

**Q4 — What does a company holiday party cost?**

> "Talking only about the bar — food and venue are their own conversations. Most corporate holiday
> parties we stock run between five hundred and two thousand dollars. For a fifty-person party with
> beer, wine and one signature cocktail, expect around eight hundred to twelve hundred. Fifty to a
> hundred guests, call it eight hundred to fifteen hundred. Our packaged options start at fourteen
> ninety-nine for an executive reception and go to about four thousand for a full offsite weekend.
> And all of it comes as one invoice, which is the part your finance team actually cares about."

**Q5 — Is the company holiday party tax-deductible?** *(picks up after the on-camera tax line)*

> "Most business meals got cut to fifty percent — but a recreational event primarily for your
> employees, open to the whole team rather than just executives, is treated differently and generally
> comes in at a hundred. The catch is in the words 'primarily for employees' and 'not just the
> highly-compensated ones.' Invite everyone, keep the invite, keep the itemized receipt, and code it
> separately from client entertainment. I'm not your accountant — ask yours before you file. But ask
> them *before* you plan, not after, because it can genuinely change the budget you're working with."

**Q6 — How much alcohol do you need for an office party?**

> "One drink per person per hour, plus a little cushion. Fifty people for three hours is about a
> hundred and seventy-five drinks. Split it forty percent wine, thirty beer, thirty spirits and
> that's fourteen bottles of wine, four bottles of liquor, and about fifty beers. Four bottles sounds
> low — it isn't. A seven-fifty gives you seventeen drinks, and at an office party a lot of people
> have one glass of wine and switch to sparkling water. Which brings up the thing most planners
> underbuy: non-alcoholic. Get two good options that aren't just soda, and get enough that they don't
> run out first. That's the detail people notice."

**Q7 — What should you actually serve?**

> "Keep the menu short. What works over and over at Austin office parties: one light signature
> cocktail — a ranch water, or a spiked Arnold Palmer — a red and a white wine, and local beer to
> round it out. Four decisions, done. Nobody misses the twelve-bottle bar, and the difference between
> a forgettable bottle and a good one across a fifty-person party is maybe a hundred and fifty
> dollars total. Your team notices the good bottles. They definitely notice the bad ones. And in
> Austin, local beer reads as effort."

**Q8 — What about happy hours and team events the rest of the year?**

> "If you only do one event a year, it carries impossible weight. A standing Thursday happy hour once
> a month, same time, optional, does more for how a team actually feels than any single party.
> Budget-wise it's the same money spread out. The format that works best: one signature cocktail
> everybody's making that month, decent beer and wine alongside, and an actual end time so people can
> leave. And for the team-building version — cocktail kits where everyone builds the same drink at
> the same time genuinely works."

**Q9 — Do you need TABC, a bartender, or a COI for an office party?**

> "For a private company event where you're not selling drinks, you don't need your own permit. What
> you will get asked for — usually by your building management or the venue — is a certificate of
> insurance, and often proof your bartenders are certified. We're a TABC-licensed retailer, permit
> P-two-hundred-eighty-four-three-nine-eight, with a million dollars in general and liquor liability,
> and we'll send a COI on request. One thing to know up front: we can't add your venue or your
> company as additionally insured — that trips people up late, so ask your building what they need
> the week you book the room, not the week of the party."

# PART 3 — GUARDRAILS

Wording that is deliberate and **may not be tightened, trimmed, or improvised** on the day. Each one
was decided for a reason; the reasons are in the source scripts' claims trackers.

| Guardrail | Why |
|---|---|
| Corporate Q5: say **"generally"** and say the "primarily for employees / not just the highly-compensated ones" condition out loud. The on-screen **"Ask your CPA"** card is mandatory. | Approved *as hedged*. The hedge is the entire protection — there's no first-party source behind the tax claim. |
| Wedding Q3/Q4: the **"(industry averages)"** tag stays on screen with the $25–45 range. | It's an external figure. The first-party anchor ($1,773) is what actually carries the chapter. |
| Wedding Q4: always say **bartenders are billed separately** whenever the $13–26/pp tiers are on screen. | Otherwise the tiers read as all-in. |
| Wedding Q8: shoot the return as **a customer carrying unopened cases INTO the store** — never a van pickup. | We don't collect leftovers. Getting this wrong on camera creates an expectation ops has to eat. |
| Corporate Q9: keep the **"we cannot add third parties as additionally insured"** caveat even if the edit runs long. | It's the thing that trips clients up late; the lander states it verbatim. |
| Corporate: **never name a licensed entity on camera.** | The entity name isn't documented anywhere internally. |
| Wedding Q10: say **"most"**, never "median." | Most weddings enter as bartender-referred private parties and are untagged — the operator count supports "most," the DB medians don't support "median." |
| Corporate Q7 + wedding Q5 drink lists: these are **operator observations**, not rankings. Don't add "top" or "number one." | The underlying pulls (n=11, n=15) are too thin to carry a ranking claim. |
| Everywhere: **wine 5 / spirits 17 / champagne 5** glasses per 750ml. | Site-wide canon since 2026-08-03 — every calculator agrees, so on-screen numbers are checkable against the live tools. |
